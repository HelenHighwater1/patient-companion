Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD) and Becker muscular dystrophy (BMD) are allelic X‐linked myopathies caused by pathogenic variants in DMD, which encodes dystrophin, essential for stabilizing muscle fibers during contraction. 1 DMD presents in early childhood with rapid muscle weakness and ambulation loss by 10–12 years of age, whereas BMD has later onset and milder progression, often with considerable variability, 2 , 3 with a subset developing X‐linked dilated cardiomyopathy with minimal skeletal involvement. 4 As per the “reading‐frame hypothesis”, 5 out‐of‐frame variants produce truncated or absent dystrophin and a Duchenne phenotype, while in‐frame variants yield partially functional protein and a Becker phenotype. Although this rule holds in ~91% of cases, exceptions occur. 6 , 7 Here, we report an out‐of‐frame DMD variant in a patient with a Becker phenotype and reveal transcriptional slippage as a novel mechanism that restores the reading frame and rescues dystrophin expression.MethodsThis study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Tokyo (approval number: G1396). The study procedures were conducted in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki. Genomic DNA and muscle samples were collected from all participants after obtaining written informed consent. A detailed description of the methods is provided in the Supporting Information.ResultsA 23‐year‐old man was first noted to have markedly elevated serum creatine kinase (CK) levels and reduced cardiac function at age 20, when he presented with exertional dyspnea and a left ventricular ejection fraction of 15%. He had no known family history of muscular dystrophy or consanguinity, and both parents were healthy with no reported weakness. Although the patient experienced no major developmental delays in childhood, he consistently finished last in running events and occasionally noticed dark‐colored urine after exercise. Clinically, he exhibited mild, symmetric proximal muscle weakness (Medical Research Council [MRC] grade ~4/5) and pronounced calf hypertrophy, but remained ambulatory without assistive devices and experienced minimal impact on daily activities. Deep tendon reflexes were preserved, and no sensory or bulbar signs were observed.Laboratory tests demonstrated persistently elevated serum CK (3,000–4,000 U/L) and mildly elevated transaminase levels; other routine blood parameters were unremarkable. Echocardiography showed a dilated left ventricle (diastolic diameter 6.7 cm) and severely reduced ejection fraction (15–23% by Teichholz), while pulmonary function testing revealed a mildly restrictive pattern (vital capacity 3.11 L, 67.3% predicted).A biceps brachii muscle biopsy revealed fiber size variation, expansion of the endomysial space, and disorganization of the myofibrillar network. Increased internal nuclei and occasional fiber splitting were noted, with some fibers showing necrotic changes (Fig 1A). Immunofluorescence staining for dystrophin (using dys1, dys2, and dys3 antibodies; see Supporting Information) revealed patchy but definite sarcolemmal staining in many fibers, albeit at reduced intensity compared to normal muscle (Fig 1B–D). This partial dystrophin staining pattern is atypical for DMD, in which staining is typically absent, and is more consistent with the pattern for BMD. Subsequent whole‐exome sequence analysis of genomic DNA from the patient's peripheral blood leukocytes identified a novel hemizygous single‐base deletion, c.2281delG, in exon 18 of the DMD gene (NM_004006.3; NP_003997.2: p.Glu761Lysfs*6). Notably, this variant converts the normal A4GA5 motif into a continuous run of 9 adenines (A9) and is absent from public population databases, including gnomAD v4 and jMorp 60KJPN, as well as from an in‐house exome database of 1,163 healthy controls. 8 The patient's mother was a heterozygous carrier of the same variant (Fig 2B). The variant is predicted to introduce a premature stop codon, triggering nonsense‐mediated mRNA decay (NMD), thereby eliminating functional dystrophin and inducing the DMD phenotype. One A9 read does not splice at the exon 18–19 junction; unlike the other 4 reads that splice into exon 19, this read extends into the downstream intron and terminates within it.Genomic DNA extracted from both biceps muscle and peripheral blood leukocytes was directly sequenced at the c.2281delG locus to explore the possibility of somatic mosaicism as a potential explanation for residual dystrophin expression. Only the mutant allele was detected, with no evidence of wild‐type DMD sequence (Fig 2C). Concordantly, whole‐exome sequence analysis of peripheral blood leukocyte genomic DNA, performed as described in the Supporting Information, showed that all site‐spanning reads counted by the variant caller (read depth = 42) at GRCh38 chrX:32,518,018 carried the deletion allele and none carried the reference allele (Fig 2D). Taken together, these DNA‐level results argue against the presence of a wild‐type mosaic fraction sufficient to account for the observed protein findings. Immunofluorescence staining for dystrophin (dys1, dys2, and dys3) demonstrated diffused distribution of the partial dystrophin signal across many fibers, rather than discrete patches, making the presence of focal revertant fibers unlikely. These findings suggested involvement of an RNA‐level mechanism that partially corrected the frameshift.To investigate this possibility, total RNA was extracted from the biopsied biceps muscle specimens from the patient and controls. Polymerase chain reaction (PCR)‐based cDNA analysis was conducted using primers that amplified the c.2281delG variant region (see Supporting Information). The PCR products were similar in size to those from control muscle but appeared markedly fainter on agarose gel electrophoresis, consistent with partial transcript degradation through NMD (Fig 3B).To precisely quantify DMD transcript levels, droplet digital PCR (ddPCR) was performed using custom‐designed primers and probes targeting exons 1–3 of DMD and SDHA as internal control (Supporting Information). The resulting DMD/SDHA ratio was 0.0734 in the patient (range: 0.063–0.078), compared to 0.160 in controls (range: 0.145–0.160), indicating DMD level in the patient's muscle to be ~46% of that in normal controls (Fig 3C).Remarkably, direct nucleotide sequence analysis of cDNA identified 2 transcript species. The predominant species corresponded to mutant transcripts lacking a frameshift correction (designated A9), while a smaller subset contained an additional adenine (designated A10), which restored the reading frame and was predicted to produce full‐length dystrophin with a single amino acid substitution (p.Glu761Lys). Visual inspection of the electropherogram showed mixed sequencing peaks at the homopolymer region, consistent with the presence of an A10 component at lower abundance than A9 (Fig 3D). To validate this finding without reverse transcription or PCR amplification, direct RNA sequencing of RNA extracted from the biopsied patient muscle (Oxford Nanopore, see Supporting Information) confirmed the RNA molecules corresponding to A9 and A10 transcript species and additionally observed a single A8 read (A10 in 2 of 5 reads, A9 in 2 of 5 reads, and 1 A8 read; Fig 3E). Alignments of the RNA sequence data showed canonical exon–exon junctions across exons 17–19 with no exon 18 skipping within the available coverage. Together with the overall reduction of DMD mRNA to approximately 46% of the control level, these observations align with the Western blot results showing approximately 15% full‐length dystrophin.DiscussionOur study uncovers transcriptional slippage as a previously unrecognized mechanism for rescuing dystrophin expression in an out‐of‐frame DMD variant, leading to an atypical BMD phenotype. Out‐of‐frame DMD variants typically abolish dystrophin production and cause a severe DMD phenotype, whereas in‐frame variants produce truncated but partially functional protein, yielding the milder BMD phenotype. However, exceptions owing to alternative translation initiation, cryptic promoter usage, exon skipping, ribosomal frameshifting, RNA editing, and somatic mosaicism have been reported. 10 In our patient, a novel c.2281delG frameshift in exon 18 converts the native A4GA5 motif into an A9 homopolymer. Genomic sequencing ruled out mosaicism, while protein‐level analyses showed presence of ~15% of full‐length dystrophin. At the transcript level, cDNA analysis identified 2 species (A9 and frame‐restored A10), and PCR‐free direct RNA sequencing of muscle qualitatively corroborated the presence of both. The continuous 9‐adenine run (A9) in the c.2281delG variant is known to predispose transcripts to transcriptional slippage. Although the direct RNA sequencing data contained only 5 reads covering this locus, including 2 A10 reads, the results confirmed the presence of an RNA molecule with transcriptional slippage resulting in the incorporation of 1 additional adenine (A₁₀). To obtain a quantitative estimate of relative abundance, additional high‐depth sequencing would be required. Nevertheless, this transcript pattern is consistent with restoration of the reading frame due to the transcriptional slippage and with the Western blot finding of approximately 15% full‐length dystrophin. This aligns with a milder dystrophic skeletal muscle phenotype, as even low levels of dystrophin can delay early loss of ambulation that is characteristic of DMD.Transcriptional slippage refers to RNA polymerase stuttering at short homopolymeric runs, whereby a single‐nucleotide insertion can, in some instances, restore the reading frame without any change to genomic DNA. Transcriptional slippage within homopolymeric tracts has been well characterized in bacteria and viruses but rarely reported in humans. 11 , 12 Comparable examples include familial hypobetalipoproteinaemia, where slippage in an A5CA3 → A8 context yields ~11% in‐frame APOB transcripts, 13 , 14 and mild hemophilia A, with slippage in an A8TA2 tract restoring ~39% of factor VIII transcripts. 15 In each, partial rescue reduces disease severity relative to complete null variants. Notably, in addition to the predominant A9 and frame‐restored A10 transcripts, direct RNA sequencing detected a single A8 read. Although present at very low abundance, this observation suggests that transcriptional slippage at the homopolymeric tract may occur bidirectionally, generating transcripts with either an extra or missing adenine. Such bidirectional slippage events have been described in other homopolymer‐associated contexts in humans, supporting the notion that RNA polymerase stuttering underlies the observed transcript heterogeneity. 13 , 14 , 15 Our patient's clinical profile—mild muscle weakness with preserved ambulation but severe cardiomyopathy—suggests tissue‐specific differences in slippage efficiency. Skeletal muscle's robust regenerative capacity may amplify the impact of frame‐corrected transcripts, whereas limited myocardial regeneration and potentially more stringent NMD in cardiac cells may curtail slippage‐mediated rescue, resulting in dilated cardiomyopathy. 16 , 17 Although direct assessment of cardiac tissue was unfeasible, the disparity underscores the need to consider tissue context when evaluating genotype–phenotype correlations.In conclusion, this detailed patient evaluation demonstrates that transcriptional slippage can mitigate the effects of a frameshift variant in DMD, yielding sufficient functional dystrophin to shift the clinical presentation from a Duchenne‐like to a Becker‐like phenotype. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized RNA‐level rescue mechanism and underscore the complexity of genotype–phenotype relationships in muscular dystrophies.
