Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) and frontotemporal dementia (FTD) are neurodegenerative diseases that exist on a clinical and pathological spectrum (ALS‐FTD) affecting motor and executive functions. 1 These diseases have been causally linked to mutations in genes encoding a variety of proteins involved with distinct cellular functions. 2 , 3 Among these are mutations in the gene (TARDBP) encoding TDP‐43, 4 , 5 that were identified following the discovery that TDP‐43 aggregation and its nuclear depletion are pathological features in some remaining cells from postmortem nervous system tissue in a majority of ALS and half of FTD cases. 6 , 7 Although mutations in TARDBP are rare in most patient cohorts (~1–4% of ALS‐FTD cases), their study gives insight into our understanding of the role TDP‐43 plays in disease.TDP‐43 is a member of the heterogeneous nuclear ribonucleoprotein family of proteins where it is predominantly found in the nucleus of healthy motor neurons. It contains a bipartite nuclear localization sequence near the amino‐terminus, 2 RNA recognition motifs and a low complexity glycine rich domain near the carboxyl‐terminus that contains most of the reported disease‐associated variants. TDP‐43 is involved in several steps of RNA metabolism including transcription, 8 splicing, 9 , 10 translational control, 11 , 12 and stress granule formation. 13 , 14 , 15 Previous research has demonstrated that its RNA targets are involved in various pathways including synapse formation, regulation of neurotransmitter signaling, and neuronal development. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 Although the exact mechanism of disease remains to be fully understood, loss of Tdp‐43 expression is embryonically lethal in mice 21 , 22 and mouse transgenic TARDBP models overexpressing either wild‐type or disease‐associated variants of TDP‐43 are toxic in a dose‐dependent manner (for review of rodent models, see McGoldrick et al 23 and Philips and Rothstein 24 ). This may be because of the involvement of TDP‐43 in autoregulating its own expression levels by splicing intron 7 of the 3′ untranslated region (3′ UTR) of TARDBP resulting in the degradation of its mRNA. 25 As such, transgenic animal models overexpressing single TARDBP mRNA isoforms, transgenes lacking the autoregulatory 3′ UTR TDP‐43 binding elements, or those that use non‐endogenous promoters do not accurately reflect disease states seen in patients.To address these issues, we have generated 2 knock‐in (KI) zebrafish mutant lines harboring the analogous TARDBP mutations that encode the A382T and G348C disease variants. The A382T is the most frequent TDP‐43 variant found in ALS patient cohorts. 26 , 27 This variant arose on the island of Sardinia, 28 where it accounts for approximately 1 of 3 of ALS‐FTD cases, and where it can be found in the homozygous state. 28 , 29 , 30 , 31 Patients carrying the A382T variant have a clinically heterogeneous spectrum of ALS‐FTD phenotypes. 32 , 33 Whereas patients carrying the G348C variant generally manifest only ALS. 4 , 34 , 35 , 36 We show that both the A382T and G348C zebrafish models have reduced survival and develop an adult‐onset motor phenotype that presents at 1.5 years of age. The motor phenotype coincides with reduced trunk muscle cell size. In geriatric fish (2.35–3 years of age), our KI models displayed loss of large (>300μm2) spinal cord motor neurons. We did not observe aggregates of Tdp‐43, however, there was a slight increase in cytoplasmic Tdp‐43 in motor neurons from our A382T model. Whole spinal cord RNA sequencing from 1‐year‐old zebrafish revealed an increase in transcripts associated with inflammation and immune signaling in both models, as well as a selective downregulation of transcripts essential to neuronal and synaptic function in our G348C model. We propose that these models provide additional tools that the ALS‐FTD research community can use to further our understanding of disease.Materials and Methods Zebrafish Husbandry Adult zebrafish (Danio rerio) were maintained and bred according to standard protocols under a 14‐hour/10‐hour light/dark cycle at 28.5°C. 37 All experiments were performed in compliance with the guidelines of the Canadian Council for Animal Care at the Montreal Neurological Institute at McGill University (Montreal, Quebec, Canada) and approved by the Animal Care Committee (AUP: MNI‐7890). Adult Motor Function and Survival Assays Free‐swim open‐field assays were conducted during the afternoon by placing individual adult zebrafish into a shallow (5cm) 30cm2 water arena held at a temperature of 28 ± 2°C and illuminated with room lighting. Animals habituated for 5 minutes before being video recorded for 5 minutes using a digital camera (Imaging Development Systems, Obersulm, Germany) with a Fujinon HF12.5SA‐1 lens. Mean swim velocity was calculated using EthoVision XT software (Noldus, Wageningen, Netherlands). Maximum swim velocity (f) was calculated by placing zebrafish into a swim tunnel (Loligo Systems, Viborg, Denmark) and performing a sprint test 38 where fish were subjected to rapid stepwise increases in water current velocity (5cm/s per minute) until failure to swim as previously described [38, 39, 40]. U max was determined using the Brett equation 41 where U max = U f + U s × (t f/t s). Where U f represents the water velocity during the last fully completed interval, U s represents the water velocity increase per interval, tf represents the time spent in the final interval until failure in seconds, and ts represents the total time in seconds of each interval.Survival assays were conducted by determining the length of time (days) fish remained alive or until ethical endpoints were reached (eg, inability to feed when food was placed into the tank or impaired buoyancy control) as approved by the animal care committee. Genetic groups were housed side‐by‐side in 9.5L tanks containing environmental enrichment. Fish health was monitored daily. Tissue Collection and Cutting Zebrafish were euthanized and vertebral columns from segments 2 to 3 were rapidly dissected and fixed in 4% phosphate‐buffered paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 3 to 4 hours, as previously described. 42 Segments 4 to 5 were dissected, placed in Tragacanth gum (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and snap frozen in isopentane (Acros Organics, Geel, Belgium) suspended in a bath of liquid N2. Segments 2 to 3 (used downstream for spinal cord sectioning) were rinsed in phosphate‐buffered saline (PBS) every 15 minutes for 1 hour on a rocking plate set at 50rpm. Segments were then incubated overnight in 30% sucrose (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Spinal cords were then dissected from vertebral columns and embedded in a plastic mold with optimal cutting temperature compound (OCT) (Sakura Finetek, Torrance, CA) before being frozen on dry ice. Segments 4 to 5, along with the embedded sections from segments 2 to 3 were stored at −80°C. Before cutting, samples were stabilized to the temperature of the Microm (HM500M) cryostat at −17°C for 30 minutes. Pre‐chilled OCT was applied to segment 5 of muscle sections and frozen using Super Friendly Freeze It (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Spinal cord sections (segment 2) were cut at a thickness of 40μm and muscle sections (segment 5) were cut at a thickness of 10μm. Sections were mounted on glass slides coated with 0.5% gelatin (BDH Chemicals, Darmstadt, Germany) and 0.05% chromium (III) potassium sulfate dodecahydrate (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Quantification of Motor Neuron Size and Number Slides containing spinal cord sections (9–15 sections per fish) were cut from segment 2 as previously described. 40 , 43 Briefly, slides containing sections were brought to room temperature before rinsing with PBS and then permeabilized with PBS containing 0.4% Triton X‐100 (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Sections were then rinsed for 30 minutes with PBS and blocked for an hour with a solution containing 100mM glycine (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) and 5% donkey serum in PBS (Abcam, Cambridge, UK). Anti‐choline acetyltransferase (anti‐ChAT) (1:500, EMD Millipore, Burlington, MA) and Anti‐Tdp‐43 (1:1000, Médimabs, Montreal, QC) antibodies were added to samples overnight at 4°C and then rinsed the following day with PBS. Secondary antibodies were diluted in PBS (1:2000) and incubated on slides for an hour at room temperature followed by rinsing with PBS. Slides were then mounted for at least 5 hours with ProLong Gold antifade mounting medium containing 4′,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA) before imaging. Z‐stacks were acquired using Leica TCS SP‐8 confocal microscope (Leica Microsystems, Wetzlar, Germany) and analysis was conducted using ImageJ. Primary and secondary antibodies and their respective concentrations are listed in Tables S1 and S2, respectively. Hematoxylin and Eosin Staining Slides containing muscle sections were brought to room temperature before 10‐minute fixation with 4% phosphate‐buffered PFA (FD Neurotechnologies, Columbia, MD). Following fixation, slides were stained with Harris modified hemaxtoylin (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) for 1 minute. Samples were then rinsed with water, rapidly differentiated with hydrochloric acid solution (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) dissolved in 10% ethanol for 10 seconds before being rinsed in water. Slides were then dipped in 80% ethyl alcohol 10 times and subsequently stained with 0.25% Eosin Y (Fisher Chemical, Waltham, MA) for 10 minutes. Following this, slides were rinsed with water (5 minutes) and then dehydrated in a gradient of ethyl alcohol solutions of increasing strength before xylene (Fisher Health Care, Waltham, MA) treatment. Tissue was mounted overnight in xylene‐based mounting medium (Fisher Health Care, Waltham, MA). Samples were imaged using a Zeiss Axio Imager M2 microscope and sections were stitched into a composite image (Adobe Photoshop). Image analysis was conducted using ImageJ. Quantification of fast‐twitch and slow‐twitch muscle cell diameters were calculated as previously described. 40 Immunoblot Assays Brain tissue samples were homogenized in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (RIPA) with cOmplete ULTRA protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma Aldrich, St. Louis, MO). Protein concentrations were measured using the Protein Assay Dye Reagent (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA) and analyzed using a SpectraMax PLUS 384 (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA). Equivalent amounts of protein lysate were run on a 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis gel and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane. Membranes were then blocked in a solution of 5% skim milk powder (BioShop, Burlighton, ON) in Tris‐buffered saline with 0.1% Tween 20 for 1 hour and probed with primary antibodies overnight at 4°C. Horseradish peroxidase‐tagged secondary antibodies were used for visualization by chemiluminescence using Clarity Western ECL substrate (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA) or SuperSignal West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA) and imaged using ChemoStar (Intas, Ahmedabad, India). Immunoblot band intensities were calculated using ImageJ and relative proportion of proteins of interest was quantified by normalizing to actin as a loading control and then normalized to wild‐type. Subcellular Fractionation Assay Whole brains were rapidly dissected from euthanized zebrafish and snap frozen in liquid N2. Nuclear and cytoplasmic fractions were separated using previously described methods. 44 Briefly, brains were first homogenized in cytoplasmic extraction buffer (Buffer A) containing 150mM NaCl (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), 50mM HEPES (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), 25μg/ml digitonin (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), 1M hexylene glycol (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), and cOmplete ULTRA protease inhibitor cocktail (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO), followed by centrifugation through a QIAshredder column (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) at 500g for 10 minutes. Additional cytoplasmic extraction buffer was then added to the flow‐through and incubated for 20 minutes at 4°C on an end‐to‐end rotator, before an additional centrifugation step for 10 minutes at 4,000g. The supernatant from each sample was collected as the cytoplasmic fraction. Pellets were then resuspended using a membrane buffer (Buffer B), containing the cytoplasmic extraction buffer, with 1% v:v Igepal (Sigma‐Aldrich, St. Louis, MO) replacing digitonin. Samples were then incubated for 30 minutes at 4°C followed by centrifugation at 6,000g for 10 minutes. The supernatant from samples was discarded and a 1% sarkosyl solution (ICN Biochemicals, Costa Mesa, CA) was added to the remaining pellets and sonicated followed by incubation for 45 minutes at 4 °C on an end‐to‐end rotator. After this, samples were centrifuged for 30 minutes at 14,000g and the resulting supernatant from each sample was collected as the nuclear fraction. The remaining pellets from each sample were solubilized with 8M urea (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) and constituted the insoluble fraction. RNA Extraction and quantitative polymerase chain reaction Whole brains were rapidly dissected from euthanized zebrafish and RNA was extracted using the Applied Biosystems PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions. Residual DNA was removed using DNAse I (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) and the purity of isolated RNA was assessed using a NanoDrop Lite Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA). Complementary DNA was synthesized using SuperScript VILO Master Mix (Invitrogen, Waltham, MA). Transcript levels were assessed by reverse transcription quantitative polymerase chain reaction using a LightCycler 96 (Roche, Basel, Switzerland) with PowerUp SYBR Green Master Mix (Thermo Fisher, Waltham, MA). Quantification was conducted using the delta–delta CT method 45 with normalization to ef1a. 46 Primer sequences are listed in Table S3. RNA‐Sequencing Sample preparation and RNA sequencing followed previously described methods. 40 Two spinal cords were pooled for each sample and biological triplicates were used for RNA sequencing. Whole spinal cords were dissected from euthanized adult zebrafish and RNA was extracted using Biosystems PicoPure RNA Isolation Kit (Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA) according to manufacturer's instructions. Samples were sequenced using Illumina NovaSeq 6,000 PE 100 by Genome Québec. Libraries were analyzed using GenPipes RNA‐Seq pipeline. 47 Reads were aligned to the GRCz11 Ensembl zebrafish genome with STAR 48 and transcripts were assembled using Cufflinks. 49 Normalized raw read counts from libraries were then used to calculate false discovery rates (p‐values) with DESeq. 50 Genes that had a false discovery rate of less than 0.05 were considered differentially expressed and were subjected to gene ontology (GO) analysis with DAVID Bioinformatics Resources 6.8 to elucidate pathways that were overrepresented among differentially expressed genes. Statistical Analysis GraphPad Prism 9 was used to perform statistical analysis. Datasets were first tested for normality using a Shapiro–Wilk test. Normally distributed datasets were evaluated for statistical differences using a 1‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by a post hoc Tukey's multiple comparison's test. For data sets that were not found to be normally distributed a Kruskal Wallis ANOVA on ranks was performed followed by a Dunnett's multiple comparisons test. A p‐value of less than 0.05 was used to indicate significance. For analysis of survival plots, a log‐rank test was used to assess differences among genotypes. For analysis of Tdp‐43 localization in motor neurons, a Fischer's exact test was used to determine statistical significance. Quantification of immunoblot band intensities where only 1 wild‐type sample was run in a gel, a 1‐sample t test (or a Wilcoxon test for non‐parametric datasets) was used to assess statistical significance and noted in each figure caption. Error bars in figures represent standard error of the mean unless otherwise stated.Results A382T and G348C Zebrafish Models Display Reduced Adult Survival TARDBP is represented by 2 genes in the zebrafish genome. The zebrafish TARDBP ortholog tardbp (encoding Tdp‐43) and its paralog, tardbpl (encoding Tdp‐43‐like) are both expressed throughout the nervous system during development and into adulthood. The first gene, tardbp (chromosome 6), encodes a 412 amino acid protein that shares 73% identity to human TDP‐43. Whereas the second gene, tardbpl (chromosome 23), primarily generates a Tdp‐43‐like transcript encoding a truncated protein composed of 303 amino acids that lacks the C‐terminal low complexity glycine rich domain. 51 Both the alanine (A382) and glycine (G348) residues are conserved in zebrafish Tdp‐43 (A379 and G347) and the creation of the analogous A382T variant KI line (fish A379T) was previously described 52 (Fig 1A, B). Using the same CRISPR/Cas9‐mediated KI strategy, we created a fish line expressing the analogous G348C (fish G347C) variant using a guide RNA targeting the following sequence: CAGAATCAGTCGGGTACTTC. Both models reached adulthood normally. However, when assessing long‐term survival of our mutant lines, we found that both homozygous (tardbp A379T/A379T and tardbp G347C/G347C) lines, as well as heterozygous tardbp G347C/WT animals displayed reduced survival when compared to their wild‐type siblings (median survival: tardbp WT/WT, 873 days; tardbp A379T/WT, 792 days; tardbp A379T/A379T, 607 days; tardbp G347C/WT, 622 days; tardbp G347C/G347C, 587 days). Knockout of both tardbp and tardbpl results in developmental lethality 51 , 53 but individual loss of expression of either tardbp or tardbpl results in reduced adult survival (median survival: tardbpl −/−, 607 days; tardbp −/−, 419 days) (Fig S1). Loss of tardbp expression can be compensated for by an alternative spliced tardbpl transcript encoding a larger Tdp‐43‐like V1 protein of 401 amino acids containing a C‐terminal domain that is 78% identical to Tdp‐43 (Fig S2). 51 , 53 , 54 We next examined if our zebrafish ALS models in a tardbpl −/− background 53 would result in a more severe survival phenotype. Although survival rates trended lower in all of our models in the tardbpl −/− background, only our tardbp A379T/A379T line survival was found to be significantly reduced when compared to tardbp WT/WT/tardbpl −/− zebrafish (median survival: tardbp WT/WT/tardbpl −/−, 607 days; tardbp A379T/WT/tardbpl −/−, 486 days; tardbp A379T/A379T/tardbpl −/−, 539 days; tardbp G347C/WT/tardbpl −/−, 565 days; tardbp G347C/G347C/tardbpl −/−, 602 days) (see Fig 1E).FIGURE 1Analogous zebrafish TDP‐43 A382T and G348C knock‐in (KI) models and survival assessment. [Color figure can be viewed at www.annalsofneurology.org]DiscussionAnimal models that faithfully recapitulate the genetic etiology of disease‐causing mutations are critical for furthering our understanding of the mechanisms that culminate in disease. Although this can be argued for all disease‐causing mutations, missense mutations in TARDBP may be particularly important to model by introducing analogous mutations in corresponding orthologs in vivo. Transgenic TDP‐43 models using non‐native promotors or those that rely on over‐expression of single gene isoforms, often alongside expression of the endogenous ortholog, likely impact autoregulatory feedback mechanisms a cell uses to homeostatically maintain TDP‐43 levels. Additionally, KI models may one day be needed for evaluating precision therapeutics that are tailored to individual mutations such as the use of corrective gene editing therapies if they were to become available and safe. Although mutations in TARDBP are rare, their study may also shed additional insight into the role TDP‐43 has in sporadic cases of ALS. Recently, zebrafish with a mutated nuclear localization signal coding sequence of Tdp‐43 56 or optogenetically aggregating TDP‐43 57 as well as the examination of phase separation of TDP‐43 condensates 58 have been reported. These models can be used to also explore abnormal localization and aggregation phenotypes observed in sporadic cases of ALS for which our understanding of disease remains poorly understood. In the past few years, a number KI models of missense mutations in TARDBP have been generated in the mouse ortholog including the analogous Q331K, 59 G298S, 60 M337V, 60 , 61 A315T, 62 and N390D 62 variants. In this study, we developed 2 zebrafish KI models harboring the analogous A382T and G348C ALS variants (zebrafish A379T and G347C) and demonstrated that these fish develop a late onset motor phenotype.The A382T and G348C variants were selected as these variants have substantial evidence for being pathogenic, with the A382T being the most frequent TDP‐43 variant found in patient cohorts. 29 However, this variant is difficult to model in the mouse as the analogous mouse wild‐type residue (P382) is associated with sensory and motor neuropathy 63 as well as ALS‐FTD, 64 underscoring the need for alternative models such as the zebrafish where the wild‐type residue is conserved with the human residue (see Fig 1). Conversely, the G348C variant has been modelled in both the zebrafish 65 , 66 , 67 and mouse 68 using over‐expression transgenic methodologies that have been used for therapeutic discoveries. 69 , 70 , 71 A limitation of zebrafish is that a common ancestor of teleost fishes underwent an additional round of whole genome duplication. 72 As such, the zebrafish genome contains a tardbp paralog termed tardbpl. This second gene encodes at least 2 isoforms, a shorter 303 amino acid isoform termed Tdp‐43‐like, lacking a C‐terminal domain and a larger isoform termed Tdp‐43‐like V1 encoding a 401 amino acid protein that contains a C‐terminal domain. 51 , 54 In the absence of Tdp‐43 expression, Tdp‐43‐like V1 expression increases both in whole larvae 51 and in the adult zebrafish central nervous system (Fig S2) with loss of expression of both genes conferring developmental lethality. 51 , 53 Although both tardbp and tardbpl can compensate for loss of expression of the other, they likely possess some independent functions as individual loss of expression results in reduced survival in adult zebrafish (Fig S1). We observed reduced survival in tardbp A379T/A379T, tardbp G347C/WT, and tardbp G347C/G347C zebrafish confirming the pathogenicity of these variants in adult fish. However, tardbp A379T/WT zebrafish did not display a reduced survival phenotype. We speculate that this could be because of the analogous A379 residue in Tdp‐43 being conserved (A370), but not the G347 residue in Tdp‐43‐like V1. When our models were placed into a tardbpl −/− background, survival of all our models was reduced compared to wild‐type, but we lost most of the significant differences when compared to the survival of tardbpl −/− fish. Only the tardbp A379T/A379T/tardbpl −/− group showed reduced survival suggesting that the survival phenotype arising from this variant is sensitive to dosage.Two methods were used to evaluate motor function in our zebrafish models. Our first assay measured mean swim velocity using an open‐field free‐swim assay. At 1 and 1.5 years of age both tardbp A379T/WT and tardbp A379T/A379T models did not display abnormalities in our measures of mean swim velocity (see Fig 2A, B). However, by 2 years of age both tardbp A379T/WT and tardbp A379T/A379T fish groups showed reduced mean swim velocities when compared to age‐matched wild‐type zebrafish. Conversely, at 1‐year‐old our tardbp G347C models displayed increased motor activity that was retained in 1.5‐year‐old tardbp G347C/WT fish, but lost in both heterozygous and homozygous 2‐year‐old fish. Although one must be mindful of comparing complex behavioral changes from distantly related species, at least 1 patient, harboring the G348C variant, was reported to present with major anxiety disorder. 4 Our second approach used a swim tunnel to increase the water flow that the fish must swim against in a stepwise manner, allowing us to derive the maximum swim velocity (U max) fish could attain (see Fig 2C). Using this assay, no differences were observed in any of our 1‐year‐old fish models. However, by 1.5 years of age we observed that both our tardbp A379T/A379T and tardbp G347C/G347C models displayed reduced maximum swim velocities. At this age we also observed differences in mass among our mutant models with tardbp A379T/WT and tardbp A379T/A379T models displaying reduced mass and tardbp G347C/WT displaying elevated mass compared to wild‐type fish (see Fig 2E). Although we do not know the cause of this difference between our mutants, previous research investigating the Tardbp Q331K KI mouse model reported increased body weight in both heterozygous and homozygous mice resulting from hyperphagia 59 suggesting that TDP‐43 variants can alter feeding behavior. By 2 years of age our tardbp G347C/G347C model continued displaying reduced maximum swim velocities, whereas a decreased trend was observed in our tardbp A379T/A379T model. For ethical consideration, zebrafish must be housed in groups and tagging individual zebrafish or isolating individual fish for long‐term study was not possible. As such, a limitation to our approach of measuring batches of age‐matched zebrafish is that we were unable to follow specific zebrafish as they aged. Nevertheless, our measures of motor function revealed that as a population, a motor phenotype arises in aged zebrafish expressing analogous ALS variants of TDP‐43.KI models are advantageous for exploring whether different variants alter Tdp‐43 protein levels in vivo. Examination of whole brain lysate at 1 year of age revealed that neither variant in either the heterozygous or homozygous states altered Tdp‐43 expression levels in the central nervous systems of the zebrafish (see Fig 3A, B). Although 2 Tdp‐43 KI mouse models have reported perturbed Tdp‐43 expression levels (ie, Q331K 59 and N390D 62 ) others including the M337V and G298S 60 variants do not appear to impact Tdp‐43 levels. To tease out subtle abnormalities in Tdp‐43 autoregulation we generated hemizygote (ie, tardbp A379T/− and tardbp G347C/−) fish models. In these zebrafish, tardbp A379T/− showed a trend in lower Tdp‐43 expression levels and expression was significantly reduced in tardbp G347C/− with concomitant increased expression of Tdp‐43‐like V1. In lysates from hemizygous fish in the tardbpl −/− background (ie, tardbp A379T/−/tardbpl −/− and tardbp G347C/−/tardbpl −/−) where compensation by Tdp‐43‐like V1 is not possible, we observed a significant reduction in Tdp‐43 levels in both models (see Fig 3E). However, Tdp‐43 levels in either the heterozygous or homozygous models in the tardbpl −/− background, did not differ from tardbp WT/WT/tardbpl −/− brain tissue. This contrasts our investigations examining RNA levels of tardbp in whole brains, which revealed reduced levels of tardbp in the brains of our tardbp G347C/G347C model, but not tardbp A379T/A379T fish (see Fig 3F). Taken together, our findings suggest that in the fish the analogous A382T variant does not interfere with autoregulatory capacity of Tdp‐43, whereas the G348C variant does interfere with transcriptional regulation of tardbp, but not sufficiently enough to alter Tdp‐43 protein levels in the brain. These findings underscore the importance of studying a broader range of TARDBP mutations as subtle defects in autoregulation may differ among disease variants and contribute uniquely to disease pathogenesis.To explore the possibility that Tdp‐43 might be abnormally distributed between the nucleus and cytoplasm in our disease models, we performed biochemical subcellular fractionation experiments using whole brain lysate from geriatric zebrafish. Generally, these experiments did not show abnormalities in the distribution of Tdp‐43 in tardbp A379T/A379T or tardbp G347C/G347C zebrafish in either the tardbpl WT/WT or tardbpl −/− background (see Fig 4). An exception to this was the nuclear Tdp‐43 levels of tardbp G347C/G347C fish, which were found to be slightly increased in both the tardbpl WT/WT and tardbpl −/− backgrounds when compared to wild‐type nuclear levels of Tdp‐43. Additionally, Tdp‐43‐like V1 levels in both tardbp A379T/A379T and tardbp G347C/G347C zebrafish were found to be increased in the brain suggesting that the alternately spliced paralog of Tdp‐43 may be compensating for the presence of the disease variants (see Fig 4C, D). Examination of the insoluble fraction of Tdp‐43 in KI lines revealed less insoluble Tdp‐43 in our tardbp G347C/G347C model and elevated insoluble Tdp‐43 levels in our tardbp A379T/A379T/tardbpl −/− fish when compared to wild‐type samples (Fig S3). Although this change was modest, we hypothesize that this could be because of the introduction of a novel phosphorylation site in our tardbp A379T/A379T/tardbpl −/− model. This suggests that in the context of the tardbp G347C/G347C model, neuronal dysfunction is not associated with increased levels of insoluble TDP‐43, similar to what has been observed in iPSC‐derived motor neurons. 73 In most cases of ALS, fast‐twitch muscle cell atrophy is observed early in disease course, whereas slow‐twitch muscle cells are thought to be more resistant to atrophy until later in the disease. 74 Despite possessing unimpaired motor function at 1 year of age in the swim tunnel assay, we observed reduced fast‐twitch muscle cell cross‐sectional area in tardbp A379T/A379T, tardbp G347C/WT, and tardbp G347C/G347C zebrafish, whereas only slow‐twitch muscle cells from our tardbp G347C/G347C model were found to be reduced when compared to wild‐type muscle cells (see Fig 5B). However, by 2 years of age, all our models in either the heterozygous or homozygous states possessed reduced fast‐twitch muscle cell cross‐sectional areas when compared to wild‐type samples. Furthermore, slow‐twitch muscle cells from both tardbp G347C/WT and tardbp G347C/G347C zebrafish possessed cells with reduced cross‐sectional areas when compared to wild‐type samples (see Fig 5C). Our data show that our tardbp G347C models present with a more severe muscle phenotype at younger ages, suggesting that this variant may confer a more aggressive phenotype. Fifteen patients carrying the G348C variant have been reported in the scientific literature. These patients present with disease at mean age of 49.4 years with a disease duration lasting 56.7 months, 4 , 34 , 35 , 36 whereas patients carrying the A382T variant typically present with disease later, at a mean age of 56.4 years of age, and have a disease duration lasting 54 months. 32 Nevertheless, muscle cells from both KI zebrafish geriatric models displayed severe muscle cell atrophy making analysis unfeasible (see Fig 5A).Examination of the spinal cords in our models (Fig 6) did not reveal extensive loss of motor neurons, with the exception of 1‐year‐old tardbp G347C/WT and tardbp G347C/G347C fish possessing fewer spinal cord motor neurons than wild‐type fish. We did observe a trend of fewer motor neurons in our models at 2 years and at geriatric fish. Assessment of motor neuron size in our models did not reveal changes when compared with wild‐type motor neurons in fish age 1 and 2 years. However, in geriatric fish we observed that our tardbp A379T/WT, tardbp A379T/A379T, and tardbp G347C/G347C models possessed motor neurons that were smaller in size when compared to wild‐type cells. In ALS, larger motor neurons are more susceptible to degeneration. 74 In the fish, fast‐twitch innervating motor neurons are the largest motor neuron subtype making up approximately 20% of the entire population of spinal cord motor neurons and are responsible for innervating the bulk of the zebrafish trunk musculature. 55 As such, we speculated that this subtype might be preferentially affected in our models. We observed fewer of these large motor neurons in 2‐year‐old tardbp A379T/A379T and tardbp G347C/G347C fish. Moreover, loss of these large motor neurons in geriatric fish were further evident in tardbp A379T/WT, tardbp A379T/A379T, and tardbp G347C/G347C spinal cords, suggesting a worsening phenotype.Mislocalization and aggregation of TDP‐43 are defining features of sporadic ALS cases. 6 Fitting with our whole brain biochemical fractionation experiments (see Fig 4), previous research examining Tdp‐43 localization in wild‐type adult spinal cord motor neurons suggests that most Tdp‐43 remains nuclear with only a small fraction being found in the cytoplasm. 43 In our tardbp G347C/G347C model, we found that most spinal cord Tdp‐43 localizes to the nucleus with a small amount being detected in the cytoplasm (see Fig 6A). However, tardbp A379T/A379T spinal cord motor neurons possessed a greater fraction of cells with nuclear and diffused cytoplasmic localization of Tdp‐43 (see Fig 6A, J). This discrepancy with our fractionation experiments may be because of zebrafish not possessing upper motor neurons and therefore motor neurons would not have been captured in whole brain lysate used for fractionating cellular compartments. In addition, rarely did we observe motor neurons with complete cytoplasmic localization of Tdp‐43 nor did we observe any cytoplasmic aggregates of Tdp‐43. This data suggests that mislocalization and aggregation of Tdp‐43 may not be required for the degenerative motor phenotype we observed in our models.Because of TDP‐43's role as an RNA binding protein, we performed bulk‐RNA sequencing on 1‐year‐old spinal cord to identify transcriptional changes that occur before motor function impairment. We identified 101 DEGs in tardbp A379T/A379T and 1,291 DEGs in tardbp G347C/G347C spinal cords when compared to wild‐type samples (see Fig 7). Given the large number of DEGs observed, we functionally classified them using GO analysis to characterize biological pathways that were altered. Transcripts involved in neuroinflammation were upregulated in both of our models, fitting with other reports of KI mouse models showing neuroinflammatory signatures including the presence of reactive astrocytes and gliosis before neuromuscular junction denervation. 60 Transcripts involved in DNA replication and double‐stranded break repair were also upregulated in the spinal cords of our tardbp A379T/A379T and tardbp G347C/G347C fish. These included mcm2, mcm3, mcm4, and mcm6 encoding members of the MCM. The MCM complex has been implicated in the dynamic assembly/disassembly process of stress granules. 75 Unique to the tardbp G347C/G347C model, we identified the downregulation of transcripts associated with regulation of circadian rhythms including per1a, per1b, per2, and cry2. Expression of these transcripts are also altered in the SOD1 G93A transgenic mouse, 76 suggesting they may be relevant to a broader spectrum of ALS cases. Unique to the tardbp G347C/G347C model, we observed the downregulation of transcripts in the GO categories of neurotransmitter secretion and chemical synaptic transmission. Downregulation of genes involved with synaptic function included syn2a, syn2b, syt2a, and stxbp1a, which are of particular interest as presynaptic release defects arise in TDP‐43 overexpression models. 66 , 77 Moreover, studies of post mortem cortical tissue from ALS patients indicate several of these presynaptic genes are also downregulated (eg, syn2, stxbp1, and snap25), validating their potential relevance to a broader scope of ALS cases including sporadic cases and individuals with FTD. 78 As processing and transport of many mRNAs involved in synaptic function are regulated by TDP‐43, 16 , 17 determining the critical transcripts underlying synaptic impairment could foster the development of targeted therapeutic treatments. 17 , 79 Although the presence of common upregulated genes in both of our models indicate some pathological overlap, the considerable differences in downregulated genes suggest that disease variants of TDP‐43 may cause unique RNA misregulation patterns. We observed that transcripts involved in neuronal and synaptic function were selectively downregulated in our G348C model and not in our A382T zebrafish model.
