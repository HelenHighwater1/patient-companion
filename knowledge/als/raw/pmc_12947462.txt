IntroductionAs the central hub of eukaryotic energy metabolism, mitochondria produce adenosine triphosphate (ATP) via oxidative phosphorylation, a process not only essential for cellular energy production but also critically involved in regulating cell cycle progression, redox homeostasis, and programmed cell death [1–3]. Most mitochondrial proteins are nuclear-encoded and post-translationally imported through translocase-mediated transport systems into specific suborganellar destinations: outer membrane, inner membrane, intermembrane space, and matrix [4]. To maintain the dynamic equilibrium of the mitochondrial proteome, cells have evolved a sophisticated mitochondrial protein quality control (PQC) system that counters multiple stressors including aberrant polypeptide import, protein misfolding, oxidative damage, and metabolic byproduct accumulation [5–7]. This hierarchical PQC system integrates molecular chaperones, ubiquitin–proteasome systems, mitochondrial unfolded protein response (UPRmt), ATP-dependent proteases, mitochondrial-derived vesicles, and mitophagy pathways [6]. Mitochondrial proteases in various compartments are core executors, playing an irreplaceable role in protein maturation, complex assembly, and abnormal protein clearance through specific substrate recognition and degradation mechanisms [7].Mammalian mitochondria contain 45 proteases with strict compartment-specific distributions, among which matrix-localized Lon protease 1 (LONP1) has become a research focus owing to its unique structural features and multifunctional properties. As a member of the AAA+ protease superfamily, LONP1 is highly conserved from Escherichia coli to humans [5, 8]. Its hexameric ring structure exhibits a sophisticated mechanical working mechanism: the N-terminus recognizes and binds the substrate, and the AAA+ domain drives substrate unfolding through ATP hydrolysis, delivering the unfolded polypeptide chain to the C-terminal serine protease active center for degradation [9–11]. This four-in-one “binding–unfolding–transport–cleavage” working mechanism enables it to efficiently remove oxidatively damaged proteins, misassembled enzyme complexes, and abnormal proteins, making it the first line of defense in maintaining mitochondrial matrix protein homeostasis.The functional boundaries of LONP1 have expanded beyond traditional PQC, exhibiting multifaceted regulatory properties in cellular biological activities [12]. This functional diversity can present multiple effects under pathological conditions, making the elucidation of LONP1’s functional switching mechanisms in different human disease microenvironments a critical research breakthrough. This review systematically delineates LONP1’s biological landscape from the aspects of discovery, structure, and function, while critically integrating emerging evidence of its pathophysiological duality across various human disease contexts. It aims to explore its mechanisms of action in different pathologies and its potential as a therapeutic target for related diseases.Discovery, structure, and regulation of LONP1Discovery of LONP1LONP1, also known as protease La, has a research history dating back to the mid-twentieth century. As early as 1946, Witkin’s team observed that specific gene mutations in Escherichia coli altered bacterial sensitivity to radiation, thereby establishing the conceptual framework for LONP1 research [13]. By 1964, Howard-Flanders et al. [14] pinpointed the radiation-sensitive phenotype to the LON gene locus through meticulous genetic mapping. A breakthrough came in 1981 when scientists successfully purified the protein encoded by the LON gene from Escherichia coli and confirmed it to be an ATP-dependent protease [15]. This landmark discovery unveiled LONP1’s role as an energy-dependent proteolytic enzyme, ushering in a new era of ATP-dependent protease research. Homologs of LONP1 exist across prokaryotes and eukaryotes, including bacteria, fungi, plants, animals, and humans, indicating high evolutionary conservation [5, 16–19]. This protease is highly expressed in tissues with vigorous energy metabolism, such as heart, brain, liver, and skeletal muscle, and its expression level is positively correlated with mitochondrial content and oxidative metabolic activity [5, 20].Structure of LONP1The nuclear gene encoding LONP1 is located on human chromosome 19 [5]. The LONP1 protein consists of 959 amino acids. It is initially synthesized in the cytosol as a 106-kDa precursor protein. Guided by an N-terminal mitochondrial targeting sequence (MTS), the precursor is transported to the mitochondrial outer membrane and subsequently imported into the mitochondrial matrix. Within the matrix, the precursor is cleaved into a mature, active LONP1 protein with a molecular weight of approximately 100 kDa. The mature LONP1 protein comprises three domains: an N-terminal domain (aa 67–485) responsible for substrate recognition and binding, a central AAA+ module (aa 486–729) that binds and hydrolyzes ATP, and a C-terminal domain (aa 730–959) containing the serine/lysine dyad proteolytic active site (P-domain) [9] (Fig. 1A). The full-length three-dimensional structure of LONP1 was first resolved in 2016 by the Kereïche team using cryo-electron microscopy, revealing its unique hexameric architecture: the P-domains form a bowl-shaped cavity, topped by the AAA+ modules, while the N-terminal domains create a narrow channel [21] (Fig. 1B). According to the Human Protein Atlas database (http://www.proteinatlas.org/), LONP1 is widely expressed in human tissues, and the expression level of LONP1 messenger RNA (mRNA) is relatively high in the adrenal gland, cerebral cortex, choroid plexus, thyroid gland, parathyroid gland, heart muscle, and skeletal muscle (Fig. 1C).Fig. 1A brief summary of the structure and expression of LONP1. LONP1, Lon protease 1; MTS, mitochondrial targeting sequence; ATP, adenosine triphosphateRegulation of LONP1Transcriptional regulationThe transcription of LONP1 is regulated by multiple signaling pathways. Five conserved hypoxia-inducible factor (HIF) binding motifs are distributed within 0.6 kb upstream of the transcription start site of the LONP1 promoter. Chromatin immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated that protein complexes containing HIF-1α and HIF-2α bind to the LONP1 promoter [22]. Under hypoxic conditions, HIF-1α-mediated transcriptional activation upregulates LONP1 mRNA levels. LONP1 selectively degrades cytochrome c oxidase (COX) 4–1 in the mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) complexes through its intrinsic protease activity. Concurrently, HIF-1α induces the expression of the alternative subunit COX4-2, thereby enabling hypoxia-adaptive remodeling of ETC subunits [22].The turnover rate of steroidogenic acute regulatory protein (StAR) is directly proportional to cellular LONP1 protease levels [23]. When mitochondrial StAR is overabundant, nuclear respiratory factor 2 (NRF2) binds to two consensus sequences in the proximal LONP1 promoter, driving its transcriptional activation. This regulatory mechanism enables LONP1 to degrade excess StAR, maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis [23, 24].Epidermal growth factor (EGF) is another regulator of LONP1 expression. EGF upregulates LONP1 transcription through the extracellular signal-regulated protein kinase (ERK) and phosphatidylinositol 3-kinase (PI3K) pathways. Pharmacological inhibition of PI3K results in rapid and complete suppression of LONP1 expression, whereas ERK blockade leads to delayed and partial downregulation. This differential inhibition profile establishes PI3K as the primary driver of EGF-mediated LONP1 regulation [25]. As the core catalytic subunit of the m6A methyltransferase complex, methyltransferase-like 3 (METTL3) installs m6A modifications on target RNAs, while m6A reader insulin-like growth factor 2 mRNA-binding protein 2 (IGF2BP2) recognizes these marks through its special domains to enhance LONP1 mRNA stability and suppress its degradation [30–32] (Fig. 2B).Post-translational modificationsCrotonylationLysine crotonylation (Kcr), a newly identified post-translational modification characterized by the addition of crotonyl groups to lysine residues, is involved in multiple physiological processes, including gene transcription, chromatin remodeling, and cell cycle [33]. Reduced crotonylation at the LONP1 K390 site in patients with polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) correlates with ovarian oxidative stress, hyperandrogenism, and impaired oocyte development. This reduction suppresses LONP1 expression and activity, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and elevated oxidative stress [34].PhosphorylationThe primary amino acid sequence of LONP1 contains multiple serine/threonine phosphorylation sites. Ghosh et al. [35] identified partial matches between LONP1 phosphorylation sites and Akt kinase motifs. Further experiments confirmed that Akt phosphorylates LONP1 at serine 173 and 181, enhancing its protease activity. Inhibiting LONP1 phosphorylation induces misfolded subunit accumulation in ETC complexes II and V, impairing bioenergetic metabolism, increasing reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, and suppressing tumor cell migration and invasion [35].DeacetylationAs the principal mitochondrial nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD+)-dependent deacetylase, Sirtuin3 regulates protein activity and stability through substrate-specific lysine deacetylation (Kac), thereby participating in pathophysiological processes such as cellular oxidative stress, autophagy, and cell death [36]. Sirtuin3 directly interacts with LONP1, promoting deacetylation at lysine 145 (K145) in the N-terminal region to reduce LONP1 expression. Mechanistically, K145 deacetylation enhances the ubiquitin-binding capacity at lysine 63 (K63), triggering LONP1 degradation. Sirtuin3 knockout increases LONP1 expression and acetylation levels in murine tissues [37] (Fig. 2C).Biological functions of LONP1Protein hydrolytic activityATP-dependent proteolysis constitutes the signature biochemical activity of LONP1, serving as the defining feature underlying its initial discovery and nomenclature. LONP1 specifically recognizes and degrades misfolded proteins, oxidatively damaged proteins, protein aggregates, and some critical proteins related to mitochondrial function, thereby maintaining mitochondrial protein homeostasis. Figure 3 illustrates the process of how LONP1 recognizes, binds, and degrades protein substrates. Firstly, the N-terminal domain recognizes and binds the substrate in a non-ATP-dependent manner. Subsequently, the AAA+ module binds and hydrolyzes ATP to drive conformational changes in the protease and promote substrate unfolding. Then, the unfolded substrate is translocated to a protein hydrolysis region isolated from the surrounding aqueous environment. Finally, the substrate binds to the protein hydrolysis active site, and its peptide bond is cleaved, resulting in the hydrolysis of the protein substrate into small molecular peptides [38]. In the protein hydrolysis process, substrate unfolding and translocation are considered to be rate-limiting steps [39]. Previous studies have identified various LONP1-specific degradation substrates. These substrates include aconitase (Aco) [40], uracil-DNA glycosylase isoform 1 [41], frataxin [42], cystathionine β-synthase [43], DAP3-binding cell death enhancer 1 [44], mitochondrial calpain 10 [45], 5-aminolevulinic acid synthase [46], and more. LONP1, Lon protease 1; ATP, adenosine triphosphateMolecular chaperone activityIn addition to its canonical proteolytic activity, LONP1 exhibits a unique molecular chaperone function. Through co-immunoprecipitation combined with shotgun proteomics, the Kao team systematically identified 76 mitochondrial proteins that physically interact with LONP1. These include heat shock protein (HSP) 60, mitochondrial HSP 70 (mtHSP70), myosin-9/10, glucose-regulated protein 78, protocadherin-18, and NAD reduced dehydrogenase ubiquinone Fe-S protein 8 [47]. In an Escherichia coli model, LONP1 with mutations in its proteolytic domain still effectively suppressed the aggregation of unfolded proteins, confirming that its chaperone activity is independent of its proteolytic function [38]. Further studies in human cells revealed that LONP1 does not directly regulate the protein abundance of HSP60 or mtHSP70 but instead influences apoptosis by modulating the stability of the HSP60–mtHSP70 protein complex [47, 48].Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA)-binding proteinAs a DNA-binding protein, LONP1 binds to single-stranded DNA sequences in the mitochondrial genome in a sequence- and strand-specific manner. It interacts with sequences in the heavy-strand promoter-encoding DNA and light-strand promoter noncoding DNA but shows no affinity for their complementary strands [49–52]. Notably, LONP1’s DNA-binding capacity is regulated by ATP concentration and protein substrate levels: elevated ATP concentrations reduce its DNA-binding affinity, whereas increased protein substrate levels enhance its binding strength by approximately 3.5-fold [50].Mitochondrial transcription factor A (TFAM) is a central regulator of mtDNA transcription activation and copy number in mammals. When TFAM is phosphorylated by cyclic adenosine monophosphate (cAMP)-dependent protein kinase, its ability to bind mtDNA and activate transcription is diminished, leading to the rapid and selective degradation of unbound TFAM by LONP1 [53]. LONP1 expression increases in hypoxic-induced cardiomyocytes, promoting cell apoptosis. Knockdown of LONP1 attenuates hypoxic-induced myocardial cell apoptosis by reducing ROS levels. Under normoxic conditions, overexpression of LONP1 can stimulate the production of ROS and induce cell apoptosis [57]. As a multisubunit oxidase in the mitochondrial ETC complexes, COX plays a critical role in regulating ATP production [58]. In rodent and rabbit models of hypoxia and myocardial I/R, LONP1 shows a time-dependent upregulation at both the transcriptional and translational levels. Under hypoxic conditions, phosphorylation of COX subunits (I, IVi1, and Vb) by protein kinase A (PKA) significantly increases, and these phosphorylated products are immediately recognized and degraded by LONP1 [59, 60]. Additionally, LONP1 is involved in the regulation of the stability of pyruvate dehydrogenase (PDH) kinase 4 (PDK4), a key enzyme in energy metabolism. Upregulation of PDK4 is associated with the progression of myocardial I/R injury, while its downregulation can protect against damage by inhibiting lactate production. As a short-lived protein, PDK4 is a degradation substrate of LONP1 [61, 62].Heart failureAnalysis of myocardial samples from patients with ischemic and dilated cardiomyopathy in the terminal stage of heart failure, as well as from heart donors without heart disease, found that the downregulation of multiple mitochondrial quality control genes, including LONP1, is closely related to heart failure [63]. Mitochondria-associated endoplasmic reticulum (ER) membrane (MAM) serves as a dynamic regulatory hub for the interaction between mitochondria and ER, mediating lipid exchange, calcium signaling, and mitochondrial morphology maintenance [64]. Previous studies have shown that the destruction of the MAM structure is an important factor in the development of heart failure [65]. Li et al. [66] first revealed that LONP1 is a critical anchoring protein for MAM in cardiomyocytes, with its absence leading to reduced MAM formation and mitochondrial fragmentation. Mechanistically, LONP1 ablation in cardiomyocytes promotes optic atrophy 1 (OPA1) processing (from L-OPA1 to S-OPA1) and dynamin-related protein 1 (Drp1) expression, while reducing mitofusin 1 (MFN1) expression, thereby enhancing mitochondrial fission. In addition, LONP1 ablation resulted in decreased expression levels of mitochondrial complexes I, II, III, and IV subunits. Concurrently, it led to the upregulation of various key metabolic catalytic enzymes, promoting gluconeogenesis, aspartate and glutamate metabolism, as well as amino acid biosynthesis. These metabolic alterations collectively induced metabolic reprogramming and pathological cardiac remodeling, ultimately culminating in heart failure. In a mouse model of pressure overload heart failure after transverse aortic constriction, LONP1 shows increased tyrosine nitration and carbonylation, directly impairing its proteolytic activity. In the later stages of pressure overload, administration of the mitochondrial-targeted superoxide dismutase (SOD) mimetic mitoTEMPO can partially restore mitochondrial respiration and cardiac contractility by partially restoring LONP1 activity, but in vivo knockdown of LONP1 can partially counteract this protective effect [67].Cardiac developmental disordersThe normal development of the heart depends on the precise regulation of LONP1. During the differentiation of human induced pluripotent stem cells into cardiomyocytes, LONP1 expression increases by 3.2 times, facilitating glucose metabolism by inhibiting fatty acid oxidation pathways. These findings thereby identify LONP1 as a pivotal target for optimizing the maturation of cardiomyocytes derived from human induced pluripotent stem cells [68]. Notably, embryonic cardiomyocytes lacking LONP1 exhibit accumulation of abnormal mitochondrial protein aggregates, which subsequently activate the phosphorylation of eukaryotic initiation factor 2α (eIF2α)-transcription factor 4 (ATF4) signaling axis to trigger compensatory metabolic remodeling: ATF4 upregulates the expression of glucose transporter 1 (GLUT1) to enhance glycolytic energy production; concurrently, it suppresses TFAM transcription, resulting in reduced mtDNA replication and ETC complex assembly defects. This cascade ultimately leads to ROS production and DNA damage, culminating in cardiomyocyte proliferation arrest and embryonic death [69].AtherosclerosisA recent study revealed that the interaction between heme oxygenase 1 (HMOX1) and lactate dehydrogenase B (LDHB) enhances LONP1-mediated selective degradation of mitochondrial TFAM, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and macrophage death. Administration of the LONP1 inhibitor bortezomib restores mitochondrial homeostasis in foam macrophages and alleviates atherosclerosis [70]. Compared with healthy individuals, the level of LONP1 mRNA in the lung tissue of patients with IPF is significantly reduced. Knockdown of LONP1 in alveolar type 2 (AT2) cells induces the expression of senescence-associated secretory phenotype factors [i.e., plasminogen activator inhibitor-1 (PAI-1) and transforming growth factor-β (TGF-β)], as well as senescence markers (such as p53 and p21), and leads to G2 phase cell cycle arrest [71]. Mechanism research reveals that LONP1 participates in COPD and influenza virus infection-induced airway remodeling by precisely regulating the differentiation fate of progenitor cells. Airway epithelial-specific knockout of LONP1 in mice results in the loss of differentiation capacity of primitive airway progenitor cells into club cells and ciliated cells, instead driving their differentiation into dysfunctional luminal cells. In LONP1-mutated ciliated cells, inactivation of LONP1 triggers the protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK)–eIF2α–ATF4 cascade, activating the ATF4-driven integrated stress response (ISR) and inducing ciliated cell death. Three variants (p.Thr638Met, p.Gly708Ser, and p.Arg755Gly) were repeatedly and independently discovered in unrelated families. The researchers performed Sanger sequencing on patients with CODAS and identified mutations in the LONP1 gene, specifically c.2026C > T/p.Pro676Ser, c.2161C > G/p.Arg721Gly, and c.1892C > A/c.2171C > T/p.Ser631Tyr/p.Ala724Pal [38]. There is a significant decrease in COX subunit II and changes in respiratory capacity, leading to impaired mitochondrial homeostasis and energy production [79].StrokeResearch has revealed dynamic regulatory features of LONP1 during cerebral hypoxic stress: hypoxia treatment significantly upregulates LONP1 mRNA and protein expression levels in rat astrocytes, with synchronized enhancement observed in brain tissues (particularly neurons) of ischemic stroke models. The cerebral hypoxia/ischemia induces ER stress, subsequently activating PERK, thereby driving compensatory upregulation of LONP1 [80]. In addition, during the I/R process, ATF3 reduces oxidative stress by mediating transcriptional activation of LONP1, thereby inhibiting cell apoptosis and maintaining neuronal survival. Elucidating the ATF3–LONP1 pathway provides promising insights into potential therapeutic strategies for stroke [81]. Another study found that baicalin exerts neuroprotective effects during the early phase of I/R by regulating the LONP1–HIF-1α signaling axis. Specifically, it inhibits HIF-1α-mediated aberrant activation of PDK2 while promoting LONP1-dependent selective proteasomal degradation of PDK2, thereby restoring PDH activity and enhancing ATP production [82]. Transcranial direct current stimulation (tDCS), a noninvasive brain stimulation technique, modulates excitability in the human central nervous system to promote post-stroke recovery [83, 84]. The tDCS upregulates monocarboxylate transporter 1 (MCT1) expression and propionate (PPA) level, reduces phosphorylation and activity of phosphatase and tensin homolog (PTEN) in neurons, and thereby alleviates PTEN-mediated transcriptional repression of LONP1, ultimately enhancing LONP1-dependent mitochondrial protection. polycystic ovary syndrome; CYP11A1, cytochrome P450 family 11 subfamily A member 1.Emerging insights into LONP1 functionality have expanded its therapeutic relevance for human diseases. For primary LONP1-related diseases, therapeutic strategies primarily center on gene therapy and mitochondrial function protection. This includes delivering wild-type LONP1 via adeno-associated virus vectors or employing gene editing to correct pathogenic variants. Furthermore, given the phenotypic overlap with classic mitochondrial diseases, mitochondrial protective agents are candidates for cross-indication applications. These include everolimus, a mechanistic target of rapamycin complex 1 inhibitor that modulates mitochondrial turnover, and idebenone, which bypasses complex I defects to directly transfer electrons. However, their efficacy in improving mitochondrial function remains unvalidated in patient-derived cell models of LONP1-related diseases. For secondary LONP1-related diseases, therapeutic targets can extend beyond LONP1 itself to upstream and downstream pathways, including MPC1 agonists for metabolic reprogramming, CYP11A1 inhibitors for hormone synthesis regulation, or TGF-β inhibitors for fibrosis modulation.Furthermore, the regulation of LONP1 is a double-edged sword, presenting both opportunities and challenges across different disease contexts. Specifically, LONP1 overexpression contributes to tumor aggressiveness in various cancers, positioning LONP1 inhibition as a promising anticancer strategy. Several identified inhibitors that compromise its proteolytic function have demonstrated a capacity to induce cancer cell death. Nonetheless, this approach is hampered by challenges such as potential off-target effects, systemic toxicity, and the emergence of drug resistance, issues imperative for future translational research. Conversely, in diseases driven by protein misfolding and toxic substance accumulation (such as certain neurodegenerative diseases and CKD, and PCOS), enhancing LONP1 activity demonstrates significant therapeutic value. Activators such as compound 84-B10 and artemisinin derivatives can restore mitochondrial function by improving LONP1’s efficiency in clearing misfolded proteins and toxic substances, showing protective efficacy in preclinical or clinical models. However, activating LONP1 also carries risks; it may unintentionally promote the survival of aggressive tumors by enhancing their stress resistance capacity, thereby weakening the effects of conventional chemotherapy or radiotherapy. Additionally, it might interfere with normal apoptotic programs, potentially promoting tumorigenesis.Future research should prioritize the development of highly specific LONP1-targeted agents with favorable bioavailability and pharmacokinetic profiles. To optimize clinical efficacy, a precise strategy, inhibition or activation, must be judiciously selected on the basis of the specific disease, its stage, and the role of LONP1. Moreover, the inherent functional duality of LONP1 mandates a rigorous risk–benefit assessment for any intervention to establish an optimal therapeutic window. This careful balancing act is crucial to avoid disrupting physiological homeostasis through excessive activation or incurring severe toxicity from off-target inhibition. As these pivotal challenges are incrementally addressed, LONP1 is poised to become a cornerstone therapeutic target for mitochondrial dysfunction-related diseases, potentially redefining existing treatment paradigms.
