Empathy: Mediating the Physician‐Patient RelationshipThe patient‐physician relationship benefits from the establishment of an emotional resonance between them – their shared feeling directed at the importance of securing the wellbeing of the patient. This resonance or sharing of feelings is empathy. Empathy plays a fundamental role in a physician's life and their ability to serve their patients. It is, however, both energetically demanding and, at times, aversive. Indeed, empathy can overwhelm the physician if they lose the ability to recognize that the patient’ distress is not their own, a condition referred to as emotional contagion. Moreover, the sustained expression of empathy can contribute to emotional exhaustion, a key driver of physician burnout. Emotional blunting, often seen in burnout, signals a loss of resonance with patients, depriving physicians of an intrinsic understanding of the human experience. The challenge for supporting physician empathy and compassion (ie, sharing of feelings of suffering accompanied by a desire to alleviate it) lies in cultivating these attributes of brain and mind so as to minimize the physician's stress while preserving their ability to both resonate with patients and preserve the understanding that the patient's distress is not their own (ie, through perspective taking).It is important to distinguish between empathy and compassion, as their roles in clinical practice, whereas inter‐related, are not identical. Empathy may be understood as an “epistemic skill”: the capacity to appreciate another's experience in a non‐judgmental fashion akin to the contemplative notion of non‐dual awareness (ie, dissipation of subject‐object appraisals). 1 , 2 , 3 Compassion, in contrast, can be conceptualized as more motivational to alleviate the patient's suffering. 4 , 5 , 6 Here, empathic perception informs but does not determine the clinician's motivation; it is compassion that directs the clinician toward action. 7 , 8 , 9 We argue that both skills should be cultivated in medical training. 5 , 6 Recognizing the distinction between empathy and compassion, conceptually and in moment‐to‐moment practice, helps prevent the conflation of “being empathetic” with “being compassionate,” whereas underscoring their complementary contributions to effective patient care.Although empathy is most often discussed in relation to negative affect and patient suffering, it is equally important to consider empathy for positive emotions. 10 Attuning to and sharing in patients’ expressions of relief, gratitude, or hope can strengthen the therapeutic alliance and contribute to mutual trust. 11 Moreover, empathy for positive affect may serve as a protective factor for physicians themselves, offering a counterbalance to the frequent exposure to suffering and thereby supporting professional resilience and well‐being. 12 , 13 This dimension highlights that empathic engagement is not only a tool for understanding distress but also a means of reinforcing and sustaining the positive aspects of clinical encounters.Emerging neurobiological research offers new perspectives on how empathy and compassion are instantiated in brain function, providing insights that could inform training focused on sustaining empathy and compassion without depleting the physicians’ emotional resources. Training physicians with respect to the neurobiological bases for empathy and compassion and their effective use in the clinical encounter could enhance the well‐being of both the physician and the patient. This review delineates work that explores these relationships, highlighting findings from neuroscience, psychology, and medical training research that points to evidence‐based strategies to support physician well‐being, resilience, and the quality of patient care.Empathy in Clinical Practice: Establishing the Human ConnectionEmpathy is a complex set of experiences characterized as the feeling of sharing with another's experience or state. 14 Empathy, Einfühlung (Ein = into, fühlung = feeling) in German was introduced by Lipps, as a contrast for sympathy. 15 It was defined as involving both resonating with another's internal state and projecting those resonant feelings back onto the other, providing an immediate perception of the others’ inner experiences. 15 , 16 , 17 Empathy is now characterized as multidimensional and is proposed to engage both emotion identification and affective sharing. 18 Empathy is constructed and modulated by a constellation of interactions between cognitive‐affective appraisals, leading to embodiment of another's experience, and modifiable by subconscious and top‐down control processes. 19 Some argue that that there are 2 distinct forms of empathy: affective empathy and cognitive empathy. 20 , 21 Affective empathy refers to the capacity to recognize and emotionally resonate with another's feelings, 22 whereas cognitive empathy denotes the process of appreciating, understanding, and interpreting another's experience, also referred to as mentalizing or perspective‐taking. The several ways of defining cognitive empathy points to the broadness of this label. We suggest that one should replace this term with reference to the distinct processes which it connotes and follow recent scholarship in emphasizing the distinct processes that jointly contribute to empathic understanding. 23 Thus, empathy can be operationalized by the ability to appreciate, understand, and espouse another's experience. 23 , 24 Furthermore, the study of the role of empathy in well‐being has been limited, likely due to earlier relatively nebulous characterizations of this construct and the potential of conflating it with other empathy‐related processes. 15 , 25 Conceptual ambiguity challenges precise measurement of empathy. Nevertheless, converging lines of recent evidence underscore the clinical significance of empathy as a cornerstone of practicing patient‐centered medicine and it is considered one of the primary motivational drivers of prosocial behaviors. 26 , 27 , 28 In light of the blossoming field of social psychology and affective neuroscience, the operational definition of empathy has begun to crystalize and higher empathy has been found to foster trust 27 to facilitate patient‐provider communication 29 and to promote well‐being in the practice of medicine. 30 Physician Burnout: Exhaustion, Depersonalization, and Reduced Sense of AccomplishmentWhereas helping a suffering individual can result in joy for the helper, helping behaviors also cause stress and negative emotions. The physician's role predisposes to stress and negative emotions. Indeed, physician burnout has reached epidemic proportions, with significant ramifications for health care systems impacting patients, physicians, and communities globally. 31 , 32 Physician burnout is defined as a multidimensional syndrome characterized by emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, and a reduced sense of personal accomplishment, resulting from chronic workplace stress not successfully managed. 33 It is a widespread issue among US physicians, with nearly half experiencing it at some stage in their careers, particularly those in high‐pressure frontline roles. 31 , 34 , 35 , 36 , 37 Importantly, a deficit of empathy is a primary driver of physician burnout. 38 As physicians face increasing workloads, administrative burdens, and time constraints, the cognitive and emotional resources required to maintain empathic connections with patients are often depleted. 26 Burnout negatively impacts physicians, leading to job dissatisfaction, strained personal lives, mental health challenges, and even suicide. It also harms health care organizations through reduced efficiency, increased turnover, and early retirement, while compromising patient safety, care quality, and satisfaction. In a landmark study, Shanafelt and colleagues (2019) reported that burnout prevalence among physicians significantly outpaced that of other professions, highlighting a troubling trend in health care. 37 In a 2025 meta‐analysis, nearly two‐thirds of 7,816 sampled neurologists across 6 countries reported symptoms of burnout. 35 The ramifications of the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID‐19) global pandemic magnified these challenges, leading to unprecedented levels of professional dissatisfaction and attrition. 38 Physician burnout may be worsening and is estimated to impact nearly half of all physicians, with prevalence rates exceeding 50% in some specialties. 39 , 40 Neurologists’ management of chronic, progressive, currently incurable conditions may predispose to emotional exhaustion and depersonalization, compromising patient care, organizational efficiency, and overall health care outcomes. 41 As in other medical specialties, emotional exhaustion and cognitive fatigue dramatically increase diagnostic and treatment errors, compromising patient safety that can further exacerbate reduced well‐being. 40 One source of neurologist stress is the devastating diagnoses they make (eg, amyotrophic lateral sclerosis, Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and glioblastoma) and their role in informing patients and their loved ones of these diagnoses. Neurologists seek to communicate to inform but not devastate. They adopt a cognitive perspective that anticipates how the patient will understand what they are told and anticipate how to respond to patient questions. In so doing, they may contend with feelings of inadequacy and helplessness, all while understanding the need to provide emotional support to patients and their families. 42 Burnout among neurologists is a pressing concern, with high rates attributed to the demanding nature of their work, extended hours, and the emotional toll of managing chronic and complex neurological conditions. This strain has been exacerbated as of late due to significant decline in an already under‐resourced specialty. 41 , 43 Emotionally drained neurologists thus exhibit exacerbated levels of depression, anxiety, substance use disorders, and suicidal ideation, 41 and, in turn, may see fewer patients, spend less time on research, and disengage from their families as unintended defense mechanisms. 31 , 40 These and related emotional challenges contribute to higher rates of job dissatisfaction, absenteeism, and early retirement among neurologists, leading to workforce shortages. 32 , 44 Combatting Physician BurnoutBecause physician burnout threatens both the physician's and the patient's wellbeing, a number of interventions have been examined to reduce it. A systematic review of such studies included a meta‐analysis of 19 studies, including 20 comparisons, and involving 1,550 physicians (mean age 40 years) to assess the effects of interventions targeting either individual physicians or organizational policies and functions, with durations ranging from 2 weeks to 9 months. 45 Importantly, whereas interventions at the individual level (eg, mindfulness meditation and communication skills) led to small but significant reductions in burnout, organization‐directed approaches (workload adjustments, improved team collaboration, better supervision, and increased decision‐making participation) yielded greater benefits. The effects were consistent across primary and secondary care and varied minimally with physician experience. The evidence that burnout responds more robustly to organizational interventions point to systemic issues within health care organizations as the principal driver of burnout. Effective solutions thus require organization‐level interventions that address structural problems, foster teamwork, and promote a supportive environment. 46 , 47 But maintaining a balance between emotional engagement and professional detachment is also critical to sustaining physician well‐being 44 and empathy plays a role. New insights into the neurobiology of empathy promise new approaches for increasing empathy to reduce burnout. Indeed, by combining organizational strategies with individual‐focused techniques, 45 , 47 and by monitoring the impact of these interventions with evidence‐based metrics of empathy, it may be possible to reduce burnout and enhance physician and patient wellbeing.The Neurobiology of Empathy and CompassionA recent, promising approach to addressing and characterizing the role and mechanisms of empathy and compassion in burnout and medicine is research to explore the neurobiological processes that underlie empathy and to develop and validate interventions that specifically target these mechanisms to increase empathy and, thereby, improve the physician's well‐being. Several excellent reviews have delineated the neurobiology of empathy and compassion in preclinical models, 22 , 48 , 49 humans, 19 , 50 , 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 and translational perspectives. 55 , 56 , 57 We provide a brief synopsis of the overarching neurobiological processes supporting empathy‐related behaviors and experiences. Neurobiological Insights from Animal Models Empathy has recently emerged as a key construct in social neuroscience. It is well established that empathy and/or behaviors that are consistent with empathy are found across species from mice to humans. Indeed, a strong evolutionary link exists between empathy and mammalian survival wherein the most empathic individuals exhibit a higher probability of reproduction. 24 , 56 A construct entitled “reciprocal altruism” explains how prosocial individuals outperform individuals with more selfish tendencies. 58 However, only the over last 2 decades has the neural circuitry that supports empathy been extensively investigated. The investigation of the neurobiology of empathy in rodents has enabled studies to identify participating brain regions, neuronal nuclei, and neuron subtypes. Importantly, studies in rodents support work that allows for causal relationships for circuits subserving empathy and prosocial behaviors. The neurobiological investigation of empathy in animal models uniquely complements studies in humans because human neuroimaging studies prevent interrogation of single neurons. The integration of findings in rodent models and humans have thus provided profound insights into the neural circuits and molecular mechanisms underlying empathy, highlighting conserved processes across species and offer a framework for how empathy is related to prosocial behaviors (ie, “other‐benefiting behaviors”). 22 Whereas empathy in rodents can only be inferred, their responses in experimental paradigms wherein they demonstrate responsive prosocial behaviors (ie, grooming) implicate empathic processing. Empathetic processes in rodents are largely driven by olfactory cues. 59 , 60 , 61 , 62 Rodents reliably demonstrate social transfer of affective states and corresponding physiological processes supportive of pain, 63 fear, 64 and negative emotions. 65 Rodent empathy research thus focuses on emotional contagion, 66 observational fear, 64 and prosocial behavior. 22 One set of preclinical models address emotional contagion and mimicry, the most reflexive forms of empathetic responding. Emotional contagion is when one's emotional state (fear and joy) directly triggers paralleling emotions in another individual. 48 , 67 Mirroring refers to the process by which an individual unconsciously reflects or mimics the emotions, behaviors, or physical and sensory expressions of another animal (ie, contagious yawning). 60 , 68 Mirroring is believed to foster emotional connection and understanding between individuals. The discovery of so‐called “mirror neurons” in the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), 69 , 70 premotor and primary motor cortices and subsequently in humans 68 has been instrumental in launching the biological identification of processes that reflect low‐level empathy.The second approach to rodent empathy measures assesses prosocial, helping‐related behaviors by an observer of another rodent exposed to stress with the goal of assuaging the sufferers distress. 71 Prosocial behaviors, such as freeing trapped conspecifics and allogrooming offer evidence of advanced empathic responses 48 There are, of course, differences in the way different species process empathy and facilitate prosocial behaviors.The ACC has consistently been found to serve as a neural hub for empathy‐like behaviors in rodent models. The ACC is implicated in empathic processes, including salience, 72 pain, 52 and affect. 73 In a recent seminal study, 74 Hong and colleagues identified distinct populations of rodent ACC neurons responsible for encoding and transmitting socially relevant information during observational learning and emotional contagion. 22 These neurons exhibited increased activity when rodents observed conspecific distress of another. Light‐induced modulation of neuronal activity, using optogenetic inhibition of ACC projections to the basolateral amygdala (BLA), significantly disrupted observational fear responses, 66 indicating a causal role for the ACC. The amygdala, particularly the basolateral amygdala, interacts with the ACC to process social and affective signals. Smith and colleagues emphasize the importance of the circuit involving the amygdala, ACC, and nucleus accumbens for integrating sensory and emotional information supporting social transfer of pain. 62 , 75 The central nucleus of the amygdala, through its connections to the hypothalamus and periaqueductal grey (PAG), mediates autonomic and behavioral responses to observed distress, facilitating social bonding and defense behaviors. 64 ACC‐PAG projections are necessary for helping behaviors. 76 The findings thus support the ACC as a hub for integrating the activities of nuclei critical for empathic function. The ACC's role in rodents aligns with findings in primates and humans, underscoring the importance of ACC function in empathy during evolution. Neurobiology of Human Empathy: Elaborating on the Rodent Model Most studies of empathy in humans have used functional magnetic resonance imaging (fMRI) and carefully designed interventions in which patients are exposed to stimuli aimed to induce empathy and empathic reasoning. Human studies point to additional brain regions and circuits that mediate the cognitive‐affective appraisals that underlie empathy. Studies of empathy for pain routinely point to increased activity in the anterior insula and mid‐cingulate cortex (aMCC). 19 , 52 , 77 , 78 , 79 , 80 , 81 , 82 , 83 , 84 , 85 , 86 , 87 , 88 The anterior insula contributes to empathy by facilitating interoceptive awareness, which enables individuals to perceive and integrate internal bodily states. Remarkably, it is active during the perception of pain or distress in oneself and/or other individuals. 52 , 81 , 89 , 90 , 91 , 92 The overlap in anterior insula activations for pain in both the self and in the observed other strongly support its role in empathy and affective sharing. 19 , 81 , 89 , 93 , 94 , 95 , 96 The ACC complements this by monitoring and modulating affective experiences to adjust behavior accordingly. 97 It is also critical for empathic concern and caring for others’ well‐being. Together, the anterior insula and ACC integrate the detection of emotionally salient stimuli with the generation of appropriate affective and behavioral responses, linking emotional sharing with cognitive evaluation and regulation. 19 , 94 Their co‐activation supports both affective and cognitive aspects of empathy, and disruptions in these regions are associated with conditions like psychopathy and autism spectrum disorders, which involve impaired empathic abilities. 87 , 98 , 99 , 100 , 101 , 102 , 103 Patients with insular lesions have difficult recognizing expressions in others and the affective salience of another's experience. 91 , 96 , 104 , 105 These observations are consistent with the participation of the anterior insula and ACC in the salience network, suggesting that empathy may constitute a highly salient experience. 93 Humans with damage to the inferior frontal cortex report a reduced ability to experience the emotions of others compared with healthy controls. 106 Stimulation of the inferior frontal cortex can modulate the degree to which observers perceive emotions in response to others’ touch. 107 Other prefrontal cortical regions, including the medial PFC (mPFC) and orbitofrontal cortex (OFC), are associated with recognition of another's affect. 108 , 109 , 110 Similar to animal studies, the amygdala, a central processer of emotions, especially negative affect, is critically involved in empathy and recognizing another's experience. 89 , 94 , 96 , 111 , 112 The Human Theory of Mind Neural Network In humans, the temporal parietal junction (TPJ) and its corresponding connections have been found to play a pivotal role in the cognitive and affective processes underlying empathy, in particular, with respect to the psychological construct “theory of mind.” Theory of mind refers to the ability to understand that others have thoughts, beliefs, desires, and perspectives. 113 This differs from empathy which better reflects the ability to understand another person's emotional state or perspective. Theory of mind enables individuals to understand that others uphold perspectives that may differ from their own. This capacity is essential for navigating social interactions and deciphering empathetic experiences, as it allows for predicting and interpreting others’ behavior based on their mental states. The TPJ serves as a key hub for integrating self‐other distinctions to facilitate affective sharing and theory of mind processing. 93 , 114 , 115 Critical for empathy, the TPJ supports mentalizing, enabling individuals to infer the mental states and intentions of others. 116 Neuroimaging studies consistently highlight the TPJ's involvement in tasks requiring theory of mind, such as imagining another's emotional experience or evaluating their point of view that are critical for empathetic experiences. 85 , 117 , 118 , 119 , 120 , 121 , 122 Whereas some have postulated that empathy and theory of mind are operationally distinct, 123 , 124 lesion studies underscore their links as damage to the TPJ is associated with deficits in empathy, including difficulties in adopting others’ perspectives and an increased tendency for egocentric bias. 94 The TPJ acts in concert with the mPFC and the anterior insula to facilitate integration of cognitive and affective components of empathy. 83 , 93 , 114 , 125 , 126 , 127 , 128 , 129 Functional connectivity between the right anterior insula and right supramarginal gyrus has also been found 96 , 104 to track emotional responses to another's pain. The precise role of the TPJ in modulating empathy remains an active area of investigation, with emerging evidence suggesting its involvement in balancing self‐focused and other‐focused attention during social interactions. 130 , 131 , 132 , 133 Human research has also shown that the TPJ and precuneus act together to embody another individual's pain experience. 92 The Self and Empathy The default mode network (DMN) is characterized by oscillating neural activation among the mPFC, posterior cingulate cortex (PCC)/precuneus, and inferior‐lateral temporal cortices. 134 , 135 , 136 It is critically involved in self‐referential processes. 137 , 138 , 139 , 140 , 141 The precuneus, functionally connected to V1 visual cortex, 142 facilitates self‐referential processing and ego‐centric appraisals. 143 , 144 , 145 The precuneus also integrates multisystem representations corresponding to self. 144 Recent work, using intracranial recordings in humans, shows that the precuneus explicitly processes somatic‐based self‐embodied referential experience. 144 Affective mentalizing of another individual's experience is also centrally processed by the precuneus. 66 , 94 Recent work from our laboratory found that higher cerebral blood flow was exhibited in the TPJ, precuneus, amygdala, and visual cortex when viewing a romantic partner experience pain, as compared to a stranger. 93 Interestingly, higher empathy ratings were associated with greater activation in the precuneus and primary visual cortex. Multimodal Perspective of Empathy Taken together, we propose, in light of our and others’ work, that the neurobiological substrates of empathy comprise partially overlapping but functionally distinct systems that subserve affective resonance, cognitive perspective taking, and the generation of prosocial behaviors (ie, compassion). Affective empathy is supported by structures central to emotional salience and interoceptive awareness, including the anterior insula, amygdala, and ACC 19 , 53 , 94 , 113 (see the Fig 1). These regions facilitate the rapid detection and internal representation of others’ affective states. In contrast, perspective taking relies more heavily on cortical networks implicated in mentalizing, particularly the TPJ and broader nodes of the DMN, and superior temporal sulcus, which enable attribution of intentions and beliefs to others. 12 , 84 , 96 , 119 , 146 , 147 Compassion, whereas often grouped with broader empathic responses, is increasingly recognized as a distinct motivational process with unique neural substrates that preferentially recruits striatal (caudate nucleus and putamen), ventral tegmental area (VTA), nucleus accumbens, and medial prefrontal regions (OFC and subgenual ACC), with the putamen playing a central role in linking affective salience to action initiation (see the Fig 1). 148 , 149 That is, unlike affective empathy, which predominantly engages the insula and amygdala to facilitate emotional resonance, compassion is characterized by activation in reward‐ and motivation‐related circuits, particularly the ventral striatum, putamen, nucleus accumbens, and orbitofrontal cortex (see the Fig 1). Clinically, this distinction has implications for understanding resilience in caregiving professions, where compassion, rather than empathic distress, appears to buffer against burnout and promote adaptive caregiving responses.FIGURE 1Neural correlates of affective empathy, perspective taking, and compassion. [Color figure can be viewed at www.annalsofneurology.org]Skillful Empathy: A Tool for Addressing Physician Burnout and Enhancing Health Care OutcomesWe argue that the ability to engage in “skillful empathy” will significantly contribute to combatting burnout, thus improving patient care and well‐being for physicians and their patients. 150 We envision skillful empathy as the process by which clinicians integrate and connect emotional attunement with perspective taking. It thereby promotes a therapeutic connection without the concern that the patient's emotional burdens become the clinician's. Because empathy is a state that through cultivation can be transformed into a trait, we believe that through training skillful empathy will promote the emergence of empathy as a trait by which clinicians process their patients’ emotions without internalizing them.Addressing physician burnout will likely require multimodal approaches and significant investments in both the basic neurobiology of empathy and its applications to training in humans to operationalize skillful empathy and to decipher the impact of skillful empathy on patient well‐being. The neurobiological substrates supporting skillful empathy remain poorly characterized but with further study could serve to inform interventions directly targeting underlying biological mechanisms and in so doing to reduce burnout. Based on converging lines of evidence, we propose that skillful empathy, among other approaches, can be directly cultivated by fostering a present‐centered and non‐reactive stance through the practice of mindfulness‐based mental training. 1 , 36 , 150 , 151 , 152 , 153 , 154 , 155 , 156 , 157 , 158 Mindfulness meditation, a cognitive practice premised on sustaining non‐judgmental awareness of the present moment, can increase physician awareness of stress and of their own affective appraisals to enhance self‐regulation via mindful self‐awareness 159 and has been shown to improve a wide‐spectrum of health outcomes. 160 Mindfulness meditation reliably increases empathy 161 , 162 , 163 and enhances clinician‐patient interactions 8 , 155 , 164 to improve clinician well‐being 158 and patient 165 centered outcomes. Mindfulness meditation increases physician awareness to develop a heightened ability to recognize and respond to the emotions and needs of others without becoming overwhelmed. 166 This capacity is particularly relevant in high‐stress health care environments, where emotional exhaustion and depersonalization often result from repeated exposure to patient suffering and systemic pressures. 44 , 167 , 168 , 169 A potentially important product of mindfulness meditation is its demonstrated ability to significantly reduce the influence of ego‐centric appraisals. 170 , 171 , 172 , 173 , 174 This mechanism, in turn, increases the perceived connectivity of an individual with others and their sensory environment to promote a feeling of connectedness. 175 , 176 In a recent systematic review, randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluated the efficacy of 31 distinct mindfulness‐based interventions, with the majority (78%) using the Maslach Burnout Inventory as the primary outcome measure. 177 Notably, 67% of these RCTs demonstrated statistically significant reductions in burnout‐related metrics, with emotional exhaustion emerging as the most consistently and substantially ameliorated dimension. We assert that training to promote self‐regulation of moment‐to‐moment appraisals will allow physicians to maintain their empathy while reducing the debilitating influence of stressors on well‐being. 151 , 152 , 155 , 178 Mindfulness‐based mental training, for example, not only strengthens perspective‐taking but also supports broader self‐regulative capacities through mindful self‐awareness. 178 , 179 By becoming more attuned to their own stress and affective responses, physicians are better able to regulate emotions and recognize the needs of others without becoming overwhelmed. At a conceptual level, such a multifaceted approach underscores the interdependence of cognitive, affective, and regulative processes in sustaining physician well‐being and effective patient care. At the same time, the practical implementation of these methods at scale presents challenges, including integration into already demanding training curricula, variability in institutional resources, and questions of long‐term adherence. Acknowledging these challenges alongside the conceptual strengths of mindfulness‐informed approaches points toward a promising but complex direction for improving both physician resilience and the quality of patient care that requires further empirical investigation, including how best to incorporate and evaluate such training in the medical curriculum. Although mindfulness‐based interventions are often promoted for their benefits in fostering awareness, emotional regulation, and resilience, it is important to recognize that mindfulness is not uniformly beneficial and may even carry risks if not appropriately facilitated to recognize the subtleties of adverse experiences. 180 Mindfulness‐based awareness that can unveil difficult emotions, unprocessed trauma, or heightened self‐criticism 181 , 182 and thus, could inadvertently exacerbate the very challenges it seeks to address. 183 One concern is that increased awareness, while often therapeutic, can heighten physicians’ recognition of their own limitations, exhaustion, or perceived inadequacies. In the absence of sufficient support, this heightened self‐awareness may intensify feelings of guilt, shame, or helplessness, particularly in a profession where perfectionism and self‐sacrifice are common cultural norms. 33 Instead of fostering resilience, poorly supported mindfulness practice could amplify distress and contribute to demoralization. Moreover, when mindfulness is offered in isolation, it risks being framed as an individual‐level solution to what are fundamentally systemic drivers of burnout, such as excessive workload, inadequate staffing, and organizational inefficiencies. This dynamic may foster resentment and cynicism, potentially worsening disengagement. For these reasons, mindfulness interventions in health care should be approached with care. To minimize risks, programs should be delivered by qualified facilitators, embedded within broader organizational wellness strategies, and coupled with systemic efforts to reduce workplace stressors. A supportive and ethically informed framework can foster mindfulness in a more comprehensive fashion to optimize physician burnout optimization rather than inadvertently compounding it.Further, despite growing evidence on the role of mindfulness training, other approaches have also shown promise in cultivating skillful empathy such as compassion focused training, perspective taking interventions, 184 , 185 and interventions that use brief but high impact and digitally delivered training. 186 Although mindfulness meditation has been emphasized for its role in cultivating self‐awareness and emotion regulation, compassion‐based practices may exert a more direct influence on empathy. 1 , 153 Practices such as compassion meditation or loving‐kindness meditation explicitly cultivate empathic concern and prosocial motivation, and some evidence suggests more focused and targeted compassion cultivation training may uniquely increase empathy and compassion compared to mindfulness training alone. 187 From this perspective, compassion‐based interventions could serve as valuable complements to mindfulness approaches, particularly in clinical training where empathic capacity and sustained motivation to alleviate suffering are central. Future work comparing the distinct and synergistic effects of mindfulness and compassion practices will be important for identifying the most effective strategies to enhance physician well‐being and patient care. As the health care field continues to grapple with increasing demands and emotional complexity, integrating skillful empathy into medical practice offers a promising path forward for enhancing both patient and physician well‐being.Summary and ConclusionsNeuroscientific research on empathy and compassion has begun to play a translational role in refining and adapting interventions for clinical contexts. For instance, neuroimaging studies show that empathy for suffering can activate neural circuits associated with pain and distress, potentially contributing to empathic fatigue or burnout. 188 In contrast, compassion training engages affiliative and reward‐related networks, which has led to a growing emphasis on compassion‐based interventions as a means of fostering resilience and sustaining prosocial motivation in health care providers. Similarly, findings that mindfulness influences neural systems of attention and emotion regulation have informed the design of interventions that specifically target stress management and cognitive control in physicians. 189 Whereas mindfulness and compassion practices draw from longstanding contemplative traditions, neuroscience has provided empirical evidence that has shaped their adaptation, operationalization, and evaluation in modern health care training. In this sense, neuroscientific insights serve not only to illuminate mechanisms of action, but also to guide the development of more targeted and contextually relevant strategies for enhancing empathy and compassion in clinical practice. 190 , 191 , 192 , 193 , 194 In summary, empathy is not just an inherent trait, it is also a modifiable skill essential to high‐quality clinical care. For neurologists and other clinicians, the deliberate cultivation of empathy has potentially profound implications, enhancing patient trust, adherence, and outcomes, and mitigating provider burnout and emotional fatigue. The imperative now lies in systematically using existing, evidence‐based methods and in innovating new neurobiologically based approaches to training for skillful empathy.
