INTRODUCTIONHealthcare systems face increasing pressure from rising chronic diseases in ageing populations and staff shortages.1,2 Demands for economic efficiency can also create gaps in care delivery.3 Telemedicine (telehealth) is considered a key component for overcoming these challenges.4Telemedicine encompasses a wide range of communication technologies when providers and recipients are separated by distance or time.5 It also offers cost benefits6 and supports interdisciplinary communication, fostering collaboration across healthcare teams.7 The COVID-19 pandemic accelerated its adoption8,9 to mitigate transmission risks10 and protect vulnerable patient populations.11 The lessons learned from this have already driven the transformation of healthcare practices, including the use of telemedicine.5,12Although substantial experience regarding telemedicine has accumulated, there remains a need for research into specific clinical circumstances. Given that patients’ opinions and attitudes can substantially influence medical management, it is important to consider these factors for shared decision-making and also for patient advocacy.Much of the available data on telemedicine acceptance comes from the general population, including the population-based TAB study in Germany.13 However, it is likely unjustified to extrapolate these findings to hospitalized patients with life-threatening diseases such as lung cancer. In modern multimodal therapy, thoracic surgery and sometimes radio-chemotherapy may have a curative intent, whereas non-surgical oncological treatment often has a palliative intent.14,15 Thus, surgical and oncological lung cancer patients and their physicians might hold different perspectives, which could be reflected in their attitudes towards telemedicine.16Based on this, the present prospective cohort study aimed to assess the knowledge and acceptance of telemedicine in hospitalized patients with lung cancer undergoing either thoracic surgery or purely oncological treatment. Questions were taken from the TAB study13 and supplemented with questions specific to the hospital situation.MATERIALS AND METHODSStudy populationThis prospective, single-centre study was approved by the Ludwig Maximilian University of Munich Ethics Committee (#24-0292). In this prospective analysis, we included consecutive lung cancer patients undergoing solely surgical, multimodal, or solely oncological treatment at the Asklepios Lung Clinic Gauting, Germany, who agreed to participate in the study.All consecutive lung cancer patients aged 18 or older, admitted to the Asklepios Lung Clinic for either thoracic surgery or non-surgical oncological treatment between May 2024 and March 2025, were included. Patient enrolment took place 12 months after the official end of the Coronavirus pandemic (WHO, May 5, 2023).17 Patients treated in other hospitals, unable to read/understand the questionnaire, or those who did not have smartphones or were not familiar with mobile devices were excluded.The Surgery Group comprised all primary lung cancer patients from the Department of Thoracic Surgery who presented with a resectable tumour. The Oncological Group comprised patients with adjuvant chemotherapy upon surgery or systemic therapy in the Department of Oncology. The primary end-point was the description of the pattern of responses (study design in Figure 1). Comparisons between groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U-test for metric data or the chi-squared test for nominal variables. In some analyses, binary outcome variables were defined by contrasting “yes” responses with the pooled “no” or “no response” answers. We pooled these responses to focus on positive knowledge and attitudes towards telemedicine. The statistical comparisons were considered exploratory, only as hints; therefore, given the large number of questions, no adjustment for multiple testing was made. A P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., NJ, United States).RESULTSBaseline characteristicsIn total, 200 patients (female = 96; 48%) with a median [quartiles] age of 67.0 [61.0; 75.8] years were included (for details, see Table S1), with 100 patients in the Surgery Group and 100 in the Oncological Group. Although the overall age in both groups was similar (P = .052), the proportion of patients aged >70 years was larger in the Surgery group (49% vs 35%, P = .045). There were no significant differences regarding body mass index (BMI), pack-years, and the percentage of active smokers. Familial and educational statuses were also similar, and the majority of patients had children. Important comorbidities, whether respiratory or non-respiratory, were also similarly prevalent in both groups (Table S1).The types of non-small cell lung cancer, lung adenocarcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, and neuroendocrine histological subtypes were homogeneously distributed across the 2 groups, while a small cell lung cancer histology was present only in the Oncological Group (0% vs 10%, P = .01). As expected, UICC (Union for International Cancer Control) stages >2 were more frequent in the Oncological Group (11% vs 91%; P < .0001; UICC stages in Table S3, description of surgical approach and multimodal treatment in Table S4). Forced expiratory volume in 1 s (FEV1) and lung diffusing capacity for carbon monoxide (DLCO) (% predicted) were lower (P < .001 each) in the Oncological Group. The colour code indicates the level of statistical significance (P < .0001, P < .01, P < .05, P > .05).DISCUSSIONThis study found that surgical and oncological lung cancer patients held similar, specific preferences for telemedicine. Acceptance was high, driven by perceived advantages like saving time and reducing infection risk, despite low knowledge of specific devices and concerns about misdiagnosis and data safety. These concerns, however, did not appear linked to patients’ appreciation of the advantages.Given the rapidly rising interest in telemedicine, this study was conducted over a short period. As recruitment began 12 months after the official end of the coronavirus pandemic (WHO, May 5, 2023),17 a direct relationship cannot be assumed. However, patients might still have been influenced by the emergency measures and accelerated adoption of telemedicine during that period.The main body of questions (n = 56) was taken from a population-based German study published in 2022 (TAB Sensor, n = 1133).13 It can be assumed that the TAB study comprised many young and healthy subjects. Despite this, the responses were often similar to those in our patient population. Major exceptions were questions Q2.1.1 to Q2.1.5 regarding knowledge of technical devices (Figure 3A), which was much higher in the TAB population, although the majority of participants reported not having used these devices. For most questions, the percentages of patients providing no information were higher than in the TAB population, which might reflect a lack of information or understanding among patients.Among medical specialties, General Practice showed by far the highest level of acceptance for telemedicine (about 50%, Figure 4B), which aligns with the TAB study. This likely reflects that GPs are in closest contact with patients and regularly consulted for complaints. Similarly, about 50% of patients were willing to use telemedical support from a physician known to them in case of symptoms (Figure 5B, “acute complaints”).Despite baseline clinical differences, responses were similar. One exception was that surgical patients more frequently reported the pandemic changed their opinion on the use of telemedicine after the COVID-19 pandemic (30% vs 14%, Figure 2). This suggests the pandemic increased telemedicine acceptance in certain populations to avoid physical consultations and reduce infection risk. Correspondingly, surgical patients showed a higher acceptance of telemedicine for General Surgery among medical specialties (Figure 4B). This aligns with previous observations suggesting that telemedicine could have helped reduce infection risks during the pandemic while maintaining the doctor-patient relationship.18Few responses showed a minor dependence on age and sex. Females were more concerned about data safety, while males and patients up to 70 years placed more emphasis on shortening waiting times. No associations were found between responses and educational status.Many patients believed telemedicine appointments were more punctual than in-person visits, despite their low levels of knowledge and use (Figure 3A). Although smartphones and tablets are frequently used by the elderly, specific applications remained largely unfamiliar to many patients, and information exchange was likely limited to e-mail. Nearly 60% would prefer telemedicine in everyday life to shorten waiting times and reduce hospital stays (Q_1_8), yet only about 15% believed that telemedicine could offer the same quality of healthcare as face-to-face contact (Q_1_11). The implementation and further development of telemedicine should aim to bridge this gap.As demonstrated in Figure 6, patients’ appreciation of the potential advantages and methods of telemedicine showed a coherent pattern, lending credibility to these data, while their perception of risks was not significantly linked to their judgement on advantages. This suggests that their assessment of disadvantages was based on general concerns. Therefore, when implementing telemedicine, concerns might not automatically disappear with increased knowledge. These results support a successive implementation of various options in practice, with continuous evaluation of their actual (not just verbal) acceptance and usefulness.Telemedicine is already used for video consultations, health applications for prevention, diagnostics, screening, and apps or online therapy options.19,20 However, the inability to physically examine patients limits its applications, though fields such as ECG analysis offer more opportunities. These limitations may contribute to reports of low satisfaction among healthcare providers with telemedicine services.21,22 Thus, our findings can help identify patient opinions that are useful for focused and well-accepted implementations.LimitationsThis study has several limitations, including its single-institution design and the number of patients (n = 200), which limited statistical power and generalizability. Grouping “no” and “no response” answers might be criticized; this was done to focus on positive knowledge and attitudes, as a lack of understanding or skipping questions was not considered a positive response. Another limitation was that excluded patients could not be assessed. We also lacked a formal control group and referred to the TAB cohort data.13 Assuming this cohort comprised many young subjects, it is plausible that telemedicine was viewed more positively. This tentative comparison suggests that acceptance profiles from such studies can only be partially extrapolated to populations of severely ill, elderly patients. These patients have the greatest need for medical care and require frequent contact with physicians, posing particular challenges for telemedicine. The length of the questionnaire might also have affected the quality of the answers; a more concise questionnaire could be developed based on our results. Our study aimed to assess patients’ opinions on telemedicine, while actual adherence to telemedical devices must be addressed in future studies. Potential applications in thoracic surgery could include an outpatient tele-rehabilitation programme before or after surgery to improve patients’ clinical condition and reduce the adverse effects of surgical treatment. This topic should be assessed in large, prospective, multicentre studies.CONCLUSIONIn conclusion, elderly hospitalized patients with lung cancer expressed specific preferences for telemedicine, with no major differences between surgical and oncological patients. These preferences included using it for follow-up and its potential to save time and reduce infection risk. However, the patients’ overall level of knowledge about telemedicine was low. A detailed understanding of patient preferences is essential for the focused, well-accepted, and successful implementation of telemedicine into clinical practice.
