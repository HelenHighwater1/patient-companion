IntroductionHuntington’s disease (HD) is an autosomal dominant rare neurological disorder. It leads to cognitive, mental, and motor abilities to gradually deteriorate. Although a person can be diagnosed with HD at any time in their life, it is most frequently discovered in middle age. People may have mild involuntary movements, mild executive functioning problems, and depressive symptoms in the early stages. In spite of these challenges, they usually remain independent. As the disease advances, individuals begin to lose this independence, and their coordination and problem-solving skills also decline. Also, in later stages as motor control deteriorates and involuntary movements grow, patients may eventually lose their ability to talk, become bedridden, and require feeding tubes. This stage is frequently accompanied by severe dementia, which impairs almost every aspect of cognitive function1. HD is also linked to numerous mental health issues. Motor function may gradually deteriorate, self and spatial awareness may be lost, cognitive decline leading to dementia may occur, anxiety levels may rise, and depression symptoms may worsen as the disease advances, usually over a period of 10 to 12 years before death2.In European populations, HD affects approximately 10–13 individuals per 100,000 people. In contrast, the disease is much rarer in East Asia, with a frequency of 1 to 7 per million. Among South Africans, Black individuals have lower prevalence rates compared to white and mixed-ancestry populations3. In the Middle East, the prevalence of Huntington’s disease is estimated at 3–4 cases per 100,000 individuals4. In sub-Saharan Africa, the prevalence is approximately 2.81 per 100,000 individuals5, whereas in North America it reaches 8.87 per 100,000 individuals6.Despite ongoing research, there is currently no cure for HD, and treatment remains largely symptomatic7. Given the complex molecular mechanisms underlying HD, including impaired protein homeostasis, mitochondrial dysfunction, and excitotoxicity, targeting multiple pathways simultaneously may offer a more effective therapeutic approach. In this context, our study focuses on the identification of a multitarget inhibitor aimed at three key molecular targets implicated in HD: Kynurenine 3-Monooxygenase (KMO), Caspase-6 (CASP6), and Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3 Beta (GSK3B).The KMO is a flavin adenine dinucleotide (FAD), which acts as an essential factor and operates in the outer mitochondrial membrane, and plays a central role in regulating the kynurenine pathway (KP)8, a major route of tryptophan catabolism in mammals. This pathway begins with the conversion of tryptophan to N-formyl-L-kynurenine by the enzymes indoleamine 2,3-dioxygenase (IDO), which is found throughout the body, and tryptophan 2,3-dioxygenase (TDO), which is mainly expressed in the liver. The pathway produces multiple compounds, which include a neuroprotective metabolite, KYNA, and three neurotoxic metabolites, 3-HK, 3-HANA, and QUIN. The compound KYNA acts as an NMDA receptor antagonist, while QUIN serves as an NMDA receptor agonist to produce excitotoxicity9. QUIN has the ability to trigger HTT gene activation, while its toxicity depends on the levels of neuroprotective kynurenic acid (KYNA), which is a KP metabolite. Lower KYNA levels increase susceptibility to QUIN-induced damage10. An imbalance between these neurotoxic and neuroprotective metabolites is marked by elevated levels of 3-HK and QUIN, and decreased KYNA is strongly associated with HD pathology. The metabolic balance of harmful and protective metabolites depends on the enzyme kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (KMO), which acts as a switch. Both pharmacological and genetic inhibition of KMO successfully establishes metabolic equilibrium, which leads to decreased disease symptoms in HD models11.Caspase-6 plays a pivotal role in the pathogenesis of HD. Cleavage of huntingtin (HTT) at amino acid position 586 (aa586) by caspase-6 is recognized as a critical and rate-limiting step in the HTT proteolytic cascade12. The cleavage of mHTT by caspase-6 generates toxic N-terminal fragments that contain the polyglutamine tract. These fragments accumulate early in the HD brain and contribute to neuronal toxicity. Studies have shown that the expression of these truncated mHTT fragments is inherently toxic and their presence is associated with early neuropathological changes in HD13. Caspase-6 activity triggered by mutant huntingtin (mHTT) is not limited to the central nervous system (CNS) but is also detected in muscle tissue of HD patients and HD mouse models. Thus, it is possible that caspase-6 may contribute to both central and peripheral manifestations of Huntington’s disease14.Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3 Beta (GSK-3β) is implicated in the pathogenesis of several neurodegenerative disorders15. GSK-3β plays a critical role in the progression of HD by contributing to Tau protein pathology. In HD, both GSK-3β and its active form (pGSK-3β-Tyr216) are elevated in the hippocampus, particularly within neurons and astrocytes. This kinase is a major regulator of Tau phosphorylation, and its increased activity leads to hyperphosphorylation of Tau, which results in the formation of insoluble aggregates. These aggregates are commonly observed in the cortical, striatal, and hippocampal regions of HD brains and are associated with neuronal loss and cognitive impairment16. Despite the ethnopharmacological relevance of natural compounds for neurological disorders, their systematic application to HD drug discovery remains underexplored. Most studies investigating natural products in HD have focused on single mechanisms, typically antioxidant or anti-excitotoxic effects17, even though HD involves simultaneous dysregulation of multiple molecular pathways. Moreover, the majority of ethnomedicinal compounds have not been examined against well-validated HD molecular targets such as KMO, Caspase-6, and GSK-3β, resulting in a significant gap in the identification of natural-product-based multitarget therapeutics.MethodologyThe overall methodology is illustrated in Fig. 1, serving as a graphical abstract to simplify and clarify the study’s approach. (Created using Microsoft PowerPoint version 2512 (Build 19,530.20184).Library preparationLigands were sourced from the COCONUT database (https://coconut.naturalproducts.net/) consisting of 695,115 2D structures of ligands. The complete library was imported into the Maestro18 workspace for ligand preparation. The LigPrep tool was used to process the ligand library. A filter was applied to exclude compounds with more than 50 atoms, and ligands with fewer than 50 atoms were energy minimized using the OPLS4 force field19,20. Epik (Johnston et al., 2023b) was used to produce ionization states according to pH values of 7.4 ± 2. Metal-binding states were also included, along with the original and desalted forms, to generate a possible tautomer. For stereoisomer generation, retaining specified chiralities was kept, and up to 1 stereoisomer per ligand were generated in SDF format, resulting in a total of 321,226 ligands.Protein preparationWe selected 3 major protein targets for Huntington’s disease based on a literature search, i.e., kynurenine 3-monooxygenase (PDB id: 5X68), Caspase-6 (PDB id: 3OD5), Glycogen Synthase Kinase 3 Beta (PDB id: 1Q3W). The 3D molecular structures of these were downloaded from the Protein Data Bank (PDB). The Protein Data Bank (PDB) maintains a comprehensive collection of proteins, nucleic acids, and large biological complexes that have been resolved through experimental analysis21.All three proteins were downloaded and imported into Schrodinger’s Maestro workspace. In the Preprocess step of PPW22 terminal residues were capped, missing side chains were added, bond orders were assigned based on the CCD database, hydrogen atoms were replaced, and zero bond orders as well as disulfide bonds were generated. Missing loops were modelled using Prime23 and protonation states were generated using Epik at pH 7.4 ± 2, selecting only the most probable state for further processing24.In the Optimization of Hydrogen Bond Assignments step, sample water orientations were sampled, and crystal symmetry was used to determine the most favorable configuration. Hydrogen atoms of altered species were minimized, and PROPKA25 was used for pKa prediction and optimization26. Finally, in the Minimization and Water Deletion step, heavy atoms were minimized to a convergence threshold of 0.30 Å RMSD. Water molecules located beyond 5 Å from the ligand were deleted, and minimization was performed using the OPLS4 force field.Grid generationThe Receptor Grid Generation tool within the Schrodinger Suite was used to create docking grids for each protein target. This grid determines where ligands will dock on the receptor. The native ligand binding site was used as the active site on which the grid was generated for Caspase-6 (X:30.29,Y:6.83, Z:24.15, 10 × 10x10 Å) and GSK-3β (X: 35.12,Y:9.06 , Z:30.71, 15 × 15x15 Å). However, the structure of KMO was available only in its free state, so UNIPROT(https://www.uniprot.org/) was used to identify the residues involved with L-kynurenine binding, resulting in the formation of a grid box near Arg85 and Tyr99 (X:16.03, Y:-13.5, Z:-38.91, 10 × 10x10 Å)27.ADMET analysisThe absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity of medication candidates are all included in the phrase ADMET. The safety and effectiveness of therapeutic compounds depend heavily on these parameters. These parameters are rigorously evaluated throughout the development process21. Pharmaceutical R&D is a high-risk, multi-step process involving disease selection, target identification, and drug testing. Despite discovering millions of compounds, drug approval rates remain low, mainly due to issues with efficacy, safety, and poor ADMET profiles and toxicity. Therefore, thorough ADMET analysis plays a crucial role in reducing late-stage drug failures and improving the success rate of drug development28.We utilised QikProp29 to generate descriptors for all prepared molecules, allowing us to filter out molecules with desired properties such as CNS activity (CNS > 0) and ability to cross blood–brain barrier (BBB) (QPlogBB > = 0), which eventually resulted in 59,650 ligands for molecular docking.Molecular dockingMolecular docking is a widely used in silico method in drug discovery. It helps find new compounds with therapeutic potential. Docking predicts how a ligand interacts with its target and helps understand structure–activity relationships (SAR)30. For molecular docking, the Virtual Screening Workflow (VSW)31,32. The module from Schrodinger was used. The prepared ligand library was selected as the ligand source, and the combined input files were uploaded with the option to redistribute for sub jobs enabled. In the filter settings, both QikProp and Lipinski’s Rule of Five were applied to ensure that only ligands meeting the basic criteria for drug-likeness were forwarded to the subsequent screening stages.Pre-generated receptor grids were then incorporated into the workflow. Docking calculations in the docking tab were performed using Epik state penalties to account for different protonation states. A hierarchical docking approach was adopted, consisting of High Throughput Virtual Screening (HTVS), Standard Precision (SP) docking, and Extra Precision (XP)33 docking, followed by post-processing using Molecular Mechanics Generalized Born Surface Area (MM-GBSA) calculations. The top 10% of ligands from HTVS were passed to SP docking, and the top 10% from SP were subsequently passed to XP docking. Following XP docking energy minimization was performed, generating up to four docking poses per ligand state. Finally, 100% of the ligands from the XP were retained.Afterwards, in order to understand the flexibility of the protein binding site or induced-fit effect of ligand binding, induced-fit docking was carried out using the IFD wizard for top and potential multi-inhibitory ligand. An Extended Sampling protocol was used, which generates up to 80 poses for each ligand with automatic docking settings. Conformational sampling was set to sample ring conformations with an energy window of 2.5 kcal/mol was Prime refinement of residues within 5 Å of ligand poses was done.Molecular dynamics simulation and MM/GBSAMolecular dynamics (MD) simulation represents a computational approach that studies atom and molecule movements through the application of classical mechanics principles based on Newton’s laws of motion. Through MD simulations, scientists obtain in-depth knowledge about molecular structure and dynamics as well as thermodynamic properties and intermolecular associations in conjunction with environmental interactions. Fundamental simulation parameters, which include atomic position and velocity settings as well as system temperature and pressure measurements and atomic interaction specifications, need to be set up before beginning the simulation. The simulation is then executed over a defined time period during which atomic positions and velocities are calculated at each time step. The Desmond34,35. The program was used to simulate molecular dynamics. The System Builder program created a neutralized aqueous system by using an orthorhombic boundary with a 10 × 10 × 10 Å buffer zone, and the standard SPC solvent model, along with 0.15 M NaCl was added. The protein was then fitted securely by minimizing the box volume. The OPLS4 force field was utilised. In the case of Caspase-6, 4 Cl- ions were added to neutralise the system, resulting in a total of 33,136 atoms. Whereas in the case of GSK-3β, 15 Cl- ions were added to neutralise the system, resulting in 81,951 atoms. However, in the case of KMO, only 1 Cl- was required to make the system neutral, resulting in a total of 59,650 atoms.The prepared system files were loaded in the Molecular Dynamics panel, and molecular dynamics simulations were performed in Desmond using the OPLS4 force field. The systems were equilibrated through the standard Desmond relaxation protocol, including restrained minimization followed by restrained NVT and NPT stages before production. Production simulations were run for 100 ns under the NPT ensemble at 300 K and 1.01325 bar36. Temperature was controlled using a Nose–Hoover chain thermostat with a relaxation time of 1 ps, and pressure was maintained using the Martyna–Tobias–Klein barostat with a 2 ps relaxation time. A RESPA multiple-time-step integrator was used with an inner time step of 2 fs; long-range interactions were evaluated on the outer time step. All bonds involving hydrogens were constrained using M-SHAKE. Trajectories were saved every 100 ps. After equilibration, the systems remained stable for the duration of the production run. These trajectories were subsequently used for RMSD, RMSF, interaction analysis, and MM/GBSA calculations37. The Simulation Interaction Diagram tool examined trajectory data upon completion to measure variations between protein structures and detect molecular interactions. The MD simulation was followed by MM/GBSA analysis to calculate the binding free energy for the protein–ligand complex for every frame of the simulation. It was carried out in Ubuntu 24.02 using the thermal_mmgbsa.py script, which uses Prime MM/GBSA on individual trajectory snapshots. For every 1001 frames, and analyzed every extracted snapshot (step_size = 1). The script was run with the default behavior of deleting explicit waters and non-standard membrane atoms prior to MM/GBSA; no waters were retained. Prime MM/GBSA calculations employed the VSGB2.1 implicit solvent model with OPLS4 parameters.WaterMap analysisWaterMap38 is a versatile tool that enables research to analyse the thermodynamic behaviour of water molecules present within the ligand binding site. Using this, we can calculate the dG (Gibbs free energy) of ligand binding.WaterMap calculations were done using the WaterMap wizard, and the docked ligand was selected and retained throughout the analysis. A 10 Å radius from the binding site was selected for the calculation of hydration site energies. Also, a 2 ns MD simulation was carried out using the OPLS4 forcefield39, where water acted as solvent. In order to analyse the results, another wizard, i.e., the Examine results wizard, was used.ResultsMolecular dockingThe VSW analysis resulted in a number of ligands with high binding affinities (more than -7 kcal/mol) for each protein. Only a small subset of ligands was common to all three protein targets. Among these, the ligand with the strongest predicted binding affinity was the ionized form of 4-(1,7-diazatricyclo[7.3.1.05,13]tridecan-6-yl)butanoic acid (DTB-Acid; COCONUT ID: CNP0413439.4), namely 4-(1,7-diazoniatricyclo[7.3.1.05,13]tridecan-6-yl)butanoate. DTB-acid has a different arrangement of its chiral centers and a modified placement of its nitrogen atoms, which changes both its shape and its charge distribution, especially in the ionized form. The results are summarized in the following sections.Root-mean square deviationRMSD is a measure of structural deviation over time from a reference structure and reflects the overall stability and conformational drift of a protein–ligand complex throughout the simulation. In the context of MD simulations, initial fluctuations in RMSD are characteristically observed during the system’s equilibration and structural relaxation phase, as the complex transitions from a static docking pose to a dynamic solvated environment. In the case of Caspase-6 (PDB: 3OD5) bound to DTB-acid, the protein backbone and ligand showed initial deviations of 1.56 Å and 1.28 Å at 0.1 ns, respectively. The protein backbone RMSD reached a maximum of 4.53 Å, while the ligand RMSD peaked at 5.38 Å before stabilizing. Despite these deviations relative to the starting coordinates, the ligand maintained a stable orientation within the binding pocket with an average RMSD of $3.99$ Å during the trajectory. KMO (PDB: 5X68) in complex with DTB-acid began with backbone and ligand RMSDs of 1.65 Å and 1.90 Å at 0.1 ns. The system exhibited gradual stabilization, reaching maximum deviations of 4.43 Å for the protein and 4.17 Å for the ligand. The ligand RMSD showed significant structural convergence, concluding the simulation at 2.76 Å. The limited extent of these changes after equilibration suggests that the complex preserved a stable conformational ensemble.GSK-3β (PDB: 1Q3W) bound to DTB-acid exhibited early RMSDs of 1.57 Å for the protein and 1.49 Å for the ligand at 0.1 ns. Across the 100 ns trajectory, the RMSD values peaked at 4.76 Å for the protein and 5.41 Å for the ligand. The absence of abrupt fluctuations following the initial relaxation phase, with a stable plateau observed in the latter half of the simulation, indicates that the ligand remains securely anchored in the binding site (Fig. 3) (Table S2). Through sophisticated computational methods, including molecular docking, virtual screening, and ADMET profiling, the research identified several therapeutic compounds with the potential to interact with key proteins involved in HD pathogenesis. The multi-target approach adopted here offers an advantage over conventional single-target drugs by addressing the complex, multifactorial nature of the disease. The identified compound, DTB-acid, underwent a virtual screening process of over 695,000 natural product derivatives, followed by the induced-fit docking (IFD) of top candidates, in which only localized adjustments in side-chain conformations around the binding pocket, while the overall binding pose of DTB-acid remained consistent with that obtained from standard Glide docking. This indicates that receptor flexibility influenced the fine positioning of neighboring residues but did not alter the fundamental interaction pattern or ranking of the compound. The similarity between the two methods i.e. traditional virtual screening and IFD supports the stability of the predicted binding mode. The compound’s CNS activity score (+ 1) and QPlogBB (0.152) indicate its potential to cross the blood–brain barrier, which is essential for HD treatment. While Caco-2 and MDCK permeability were below optimal thresholds, these limitations may be solved during formulation design. Importantly, DTB-acid exhibited no violations of Lipinski’s Rule of Five, acceptable HERG inhibition, and favourable solubility and polarity values.Molecular dynamics simulations provided inference into the complex’s stability. RMSD and RMSF analysis confirmed that, despite flexibility in certain regions, the ligand maintained stable interactions at the binding site of all three proteins. MM/GBSA analysis revealed that KMO had the most favorable binding free energy, followed by Caspase-6 and GSK-3β. This suggests KMO may be the primary target of DTB-acid, although its multi-target nature was supported by binding energies across all proteins. Across all three analyses, i.e., RMSD, RMSF, and MM/GBSA, the trends are consistent. The short periods where RMSD increases, usually caused by flexible loop movements seen in the RMSF plots, do not correspond to any noticeable loss of binding stability in the MM/GBSA profiles. The binding energies remain steady, indicating that these structural fluctuations do not weaken the interaction. Taken together, the data show that the ligand stays stably bound even when the protein undergoes minor conformational shifts.However, WaterMap analysis highlighted GSK-3β as the most thermodynamically favorable target in terms of hydration site energy, supported mainly by a highly favorable entropy. The differences observed between MM/GBSA and WaterMap results arise from the distinct thermodynamic components captured by each method. MM/GBSA estimates the overall binding free energy by integrating protein–ligand interaction energies and implicit-solvent effects across the full MD trajectory, which explains why KMO appears as the most favorable binder in this analysis. In contrast, WaterMap isolates only the thermodynamic contribution of displacing hydration-site waters and therefore emphasizes entropy-driven water release and local hydration energetics. This leads to GSK-3β showing the most favorable WaterMap profile, as its binding site contains highly unstable waters that are energetically advantageous to displace. Thus, rather than being contradictory, the two methods provide complementary insights: KMO exhibits stronger direct interaction energy with DTB-acid, while GSK-3β benefits more from favorable hydration thermodynamics. The application of computational techniques significantly accelerates the drug discovery process. The study establishes an efficient pipeline using virtual screening, molecular docking, and ADMET profiling to identify and refine compounds with favorable pharmacokinetic properties, suitable for clinical development. This framework is not only applicable to HD but also adaptable to other neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s, and ALS.While the computational findings are promising, experimental validation through in vitro and in vivo studies is essential to confirm the biological activity and safety of the identified compounds before advancing to clinical trials. These results lay a solid foundation for further research and preclinical development. Also, the low predicted permeability of DTB-acid in Caco-2 and MDCK cell lines represents a key limitation, and future development may require prodrug strategies or lipid/nanoparticle-based CNS delivery systems to enhance transport.In summary, this study represents a pivotal step in the search for effective HD treatments by introducing novel multi-target therapeutic candidates through advanced in silico methods. It enhances our understanding of HD pathogenesis and offers a scalable drug discovery approach that could benefit a broad range of neurodegenerative diseases. An integrated therapeutic framework targeting multiple disease pathways is key to addressing the complexity of such disorders.ConclusionIn this study, we successfully identified a promising multi-target inhibitor (lead) for Huntington’s disease using a thorough in silico approach. DTB-acid, a stereoisomer of matrinic acid, demonstrated the ability to stably and effectively bind three key HD-associated proteins: KMO, Caspase-6, and GSK-3β. Pharmacokinetic profiling confirmed its suitability for CNS activity, and MD simulation-based analysis showed binding stability and favourable interaction energies. The combination of molecular docking, molecular dynamics, MM/GBSA, and WaterMap analyses showcases DTB-acid’s potential as a therapeutic agent. Future in vitro and in vivo validation is essential to confirm its bioactivity. Our findings support the use of multitargeted in silico screening for developing therapies against complex diseases like Huntington’s disease.Supplementary InformationBelow is the link to the electronic supplementary material.
